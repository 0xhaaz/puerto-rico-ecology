# Conservation in Borikén (Puerto Rico) - 2025 Status Report

## Overview

Puerto Rico (Borikén in the Indigenous Taíno language) faces unique and urgent conservation challenges due to its island geography, extraordinary biodiversity, and ongoing development pressures. As of 2025, approximately 16% of Puerto Rico's land—encompassing both public and private territories—is classified as protected, though this is insufficient to safeguard the island's remarkable biodiversity.

## Current Biodiversity Status (2025)

### Species Diversity
Puerto Rico hosts approximately **5,847 native wildlife species**, including:
- **190 bird species** (17 endemic)
- **54 reptile species**
- **19 amphibian species** (17 coquí species, 16% endangered)
- **5,573 insect species**
- **15 native mammal species**
- **Over 240 tree species** in El Yunque alone

### Biodiversity Hotspot
**Eastern Puerto Rico** is recognized as a critical biodiversity hotspot, hosting:
- 27 federally endangered species
- The largest remnant of primary forest left on the island
- Critical habitat corridors for endemic species

## Threatened and Endangered Species (2025)

### Government Classification
The Puerto Rican government's threatened species list includes:
- **18 Critically Endangered species**: 3 amphibians, 7 birds, 3 reptiles, 2 fish, 3 invertebrates
- **14 Endangered species** across multiple taxa
- **All sea turtle species** in Puerto Rican waters are classified as either endangered or threatened

### Notable Species Status Updates

#### Puerto Rican Parrot/Amazon (Iguaca) - *Amazona vittata*
- **Status**: Critically Endangered (IUCN)
- **Population (2021 data)**: 686 total individuals (50-200 wild, 300+ captive)
- **Historic low**: 13 individuals in 1975/1976
- **Current range**: Confined to 0.2% of former range
- **Conservation goal**: Downlist to "threatened" status (originally targeted for 2020, ongoing)
- **Funding**: Significant recovery program with habitat restoration and captive breeding

#### Coquí Frogs - *Eleutherodactylus* spp.
- **Total species**: 17 endemic coquí species
- **Status**: Several critically endangered, including *E. juanariveroi*
- **Mountain Coquí** (*E. portoricensis*): Endangered, experiencing population declines in Cordillera Central
- **Major threats**: Chytrid fungus (*Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*) increased by warming temperatures
- **Climate impacts**: Rising temperatures, changing precipitation, increased disease prevalence
- **Conservation actions**: Phase 2 recovery plans underway, expanding terrestrial protected areas, potential relocation programs

#### Leatherback Sea Turtles - *Dermochelys coriacea*
- **Status**: Critically Endangered
- **2025 update**: Nesting habitat on California Beach (Maunabo), Tres Hermanos Beach (Añasco), and Grande Beach (Arecibo) moved one step closer to federal protection in August 2025
- **Conservation focus**: Beach protection, reducing artificial lighting, marine debris removal

#### Puerto Rican Boa (Majá) - *Chilabothrus inornatus*
- **Status**: Endangered, population unknown but declining
- **Major threats**: Road mortality, habitat fragmentation, invasive predators
- **Cultural significance**: Sacred serpent in Taíno tradition

## Major Conservation Threats (2025)

### 1. Habitat Loss and Fragmentation
- **Deforestation** from agriculture, urban development, and infrastructure
- **Forest conversion** leading to significant habitat fragmentation
- **Impact**: Major threat to endemic and endangered species
- **Primary concern**: Loss of connectivity between protected areas

### 2. Invasive Species Crisis
Non-native species causing native species declines:
- **Green iguana**: Outcompeting native species
- **Asian lionfish**: Disrupting marine ecosystems
- **Invasive plants**: Altering forest composition
- **Mongoose and feral cats**: Predating endemic reptiles, birds, and amphibians
- **Rats**: Nest predation and seed consumption

### 3. Climate Change Impacts
Puerto Rico is highly susceptible to climate change effects:
- **Sea-level rise**: Threatening coastal ecosystems and nesting beaches
- **Increased storm intensity**: More frequent and severe hurricanes
- **Shifting rainfall patterns**: Extended dry seasons, altered wet season timing
- **Temperature increases**: Disease proliferation (e.g., chytrid fungus in coquís)
- **Coral bleaching**: Increased frequency and severity
- **Forest ecosystem shifts**: Potential transition from rainforest to drier, shrub-dominated ecosystems
- **Tipping point concerns**: El Yunque may reach threshold where it ceases to function as rainforest

### 4. Marine Ecosystem Degradation
- **Coral reef decline**: Stony coral tissue loss disease, bleaching, coral-killing algae
- **Coral cover reduction**: Dramatic decreases throughout Caribbean region
- **Sedimentation**: From land-use changes and coastal development
- **Eutrophication**: Nutrient pollution from agriculture and wastewater
- **Overfishing**: Depletion of herbivorous fish critical for reef health
- **Physical damage**: Boat anchors, storms, coastal construction

### 5. Pollution
- **Water contamination**: Agricultural runoff, sewage, industrial discharge
- **Coastal pollution**: Plastic debris, oil spills, marine litter
- **Light pollution**: Disrupting sea turtle nesting and bird migration
- **Air quality**: Urban and industrial emissions

### 6. Hurricane and Storm Impacts
- **Hurricane Maria (2017)**: El Yunque still showing recovery signs in 2025
- **Increased frequency**: Climate models predict more intense storms
- **Forest damage**: Slower recovery rates with warming temperatures
- **Infrastructure**: Repeated damage to conservation infrastructure

## Major Conservation Efforts and Initiatives (2025)

### Protected Areas Network
- **El Yunque National Forest**: 28,000 acres, only tropical rainforest in U.S. National Forest System
- **Guánica Dry Forest Biosphere Reserve**: UNESCO World Biosphere Reserve
- **Northeast Ecological Corridor**: Critical habitat for multiple endangered species
- **Marine Protected Areas**: Network of coastal and marine reserves
- **Current protection**: ~16% of land area (insufficient for full biodiversity protection)

### Major Funding Initiatives (2025)

#### NOAA Coral Reef Restoration
- **Funding**: Nearly $40 million awarded in 2025
- **Sources**: Bipartisan Infrastructure Law and Inflation Reduction Act
- **Puerto Rico allocation**: $10.6 million to Institute for Socio-Ecological Research
- **Jobs created**: 60+ new positions for Puerto Rican residents
- **Focus**: Large-scale coral reef restoration, student research assistantships

#### Mangrove Restoration
- **University of Puerto Rico at Aguadilla**: Coastal resilience grant from National Fish and Wildlife Foundation (NFWF)
- **Scope**: 146 acres of mangrove restoration
- **Impact**: Storm protection and resilience for three communities
- **Approach**: Innovative "Dispersion Centers" technique from Mexico
- **2025 developments**: Mangrove Action Project collaboration visit in April 2025

#### Puerto Rico Sea Grant Strategic Plan 2024-2027
- **Focus**: Research, education, and outreach
- **Goals**: Sustainable practices and biodiversity conservation
- **Community engagement**: Linking science with local communities

### Species-Specific Conservation Programs

#### Puerto Rican Parrot Recovery Program
- **Established**: 1968 (one of oldest endangered species programs)
- **Components**: Captive breeding, habitat restoration, predator control, environmental education
- **Multiple facilities**: Hurricane-resistant locations to prevent single-event loss
- **Second population**: Established in Rio Abajo State Forest
- **Success**: Population increased from 13 to 686 birds (though still critically endangered)

#### Coquí Conservation Initiative
- **Phase 2 underway**: Recovery plans for 2 endangered species
- **Support**: 14 additional coquí species persistence programs
- **Strategies**: Expanding terrestrial protected areas, potential relocation programs
- **Research**: Climate adaptation strategies for high-elevation and endangered lowland coquís
- **Monitoring**: Examining environmental factors influencing amphibian populations

#### Eastern Puerto Rico Ecosystem Resilience
- **Focus**: Endangered species protection, habitat restoration, sustainable land management
- **Target**: 27 endangered species in primary forest remnants
- **Approach**: Conservation practices integrated with local communities

### Community-Based Conservation

#### Para la Naturaleza
- **Role**: Major land trust and conservation organization
- **Focus**: Biodiversity and conservation, community engagement
- **Approach**: Connecting people with nature, land acquisition and management

#### Community Initiatives
- **Reforestation programs**: Native species restoration
- **Beach cleanups**: Coastal habitat protection, especially for sea turtles
- **Citizen science**: Community-based monitoring of wildlife populations
- **Traditional ecological knowledge**: Integrating Taíno and local knowledge with modern science
- **Ecotourism development**: Sustainable income generation supporting conservation

### Research and Education
- **International Institute of Tropical Forestry (IITF)**: USDA Forest Service research on tropical ecosystems
- **University of Puerto Rico**: Multiple research programs on endemic species
- **Long Term Ecological Research (LTER)**: NSF-funded intensive studies in El Yunque
- **NASA collaboration**: Remote sensing for forest recovery monitoring
- **School programs**: Endemic species as cultural ambassadors

## Economic Value of Ecosystem Services (2025)

### Coral Reefs
- **Tourism revenue**: Nearly $2 billion annually from reef-related tourism
- **Storm protection**: Coral reefs avert an estimated $184 million in damage annually by dissipating wave energy
- **Total reef area**: Over 5,000 km² (1,930 mi²) of shallow coral reef ecosystems
- **Coastline**: 620 km (385 mi) linear coastline

### Mangroves
- **Storm protection**: First line of defense against hurricanes and coastal erosion
- **Fisheries support**: Nursery habitat for commercially important species
- **Carbon sequestration**: Significant blue carbon storage
- **Water quality**: Filtering pollutants from land runoff

### Forests
- **Water supply**: El Yunque supplies water to much of eastern Puerto Rico
- **Carbon storage**: Significant terrestrial carbon sink
- **Soil protection**: Preventing erosion on steep mountain slopes
- **Climate regulation**: Local temperature and rainfall moderation

## Challenges to Conservation Efforts

### Funding Limitations
- Chronic underfunding for monitoring and enforcement
- Dependence on federal grants subject to political changes
- Insufficient resources for habitat acquisition

### Institutional Challenges
- Coordination among multiple agencies and jurisdictions
- Permitting complexity for restoration projects
- Limited enforcement capacity for environmental regulations

### Social and Economic Pressures
- Development pressure on remaining natural areas
- Economic priorities sometimes conflicting with conservation
- Population density in coastal areas (highest biodiversity zones)
- Recovery from Hurricane Maria straining resources

### Climate Change Uncertainty
- Difficulty planning for rapidly changing conditions
- Potential for ecosystems to cross tipping points
- Need for adaptive management approaches
- Limited understanding of synergistic threats

## Future Directions and Priorities

## Conservation Success Stories

Despite challenges, Puerto Rico has achieved notable conservation successes:
- **Puerto Rican Parrot**: Recovery from 13 to 686 birds
- **El Yunque protection**: Maintained since 1903
- **Community engagement**: Growing environmental awareness and participation
- **Coral restoration**: New large-scale initiatives with significant funding
- **Legal protections**: Strengthened endangered species and habitat laws
- **Research advances**: World-class tropical ecology research

## Conclusion

Conservation in Puerto Rico in 2025 stands at a critical juncture. While significant challenges remain—especially climate change, habitat loss, and invasive species—there is also unprecedented funding, community engagement, and scientific knowledge supporting conservation efforts. Success will require sustained commitment, innovative approaches, integration of traditional ecological knowledge, and adaptive management in the face of rapid environmental change. The island's extraordinary biodiversity and cultural heritage demand nothing less than full commitment to conservation and restoration.
