# Environmental Justice in Borikén (Puerto Rico) - 2025 Analysis

## Executive Summary

Puerto Rico faces severe environmental injustice stemming from colonial governance structures, systemic neglect, and the concentration of polluting infrastructure in poor and predominantly Afro-descendant communities. As of 2025, southeastern Puerto Rico remains a "sacrifice zone burdened with environmental injustice and racism," where communities bear disproportionate environmental burdens while receiving inadequate political power and resources to address them.

**Key Finding**: Approximately 43% of Puerto Rico's residents live in poverty, including over 56% of children, creating compounding vulnerabilities to environmental hazards and limiting capacity for environmental protection.

## 1. Communities Most Affected

### 1.1 Geographic Hotspots of Environmental Injustice

#### Guayama-Salinas Corridor (Southeastern Puerto Rico)
**Status**: Designated "sacrifice zone" for environmental injustice

**Environmental Burdens**:
- **AES Puerto Rico Coal-Fired Power Plant** (Guayama): Large coal combustion facility
- **Aguirre Power Complex** (Salinas): Fossil-fired power generation
- **Coal ash contamination**: Toxic waste from decades of coal burning
- **Air pollution**: PM2.5, SO2, NOx emissions exceeding health standards
- **Water contamination**: Coal ash ponds leaking into groundwater

**Demographics**:
- Predominantly Afro-descendant communities
- High poverty rates (>50% in some municipalities)
- Limited economic alternatives
- Historical marginalization dating to plantation economy

**Health Impacts**:
- Elevated asthma rates, especially in children
- Cardiovascular disease
- Cancer clusters reported but understudied
- Reproductive health concerns
- Mental health impacts from chronic stress

#### Peñuelas (Southern Coast)
**Environmental Burdens**:
- Coal ash disposal facilities
- Air quality degradation
- Community exposure to heavy metals (arsenic, mercury, lead)

**Community Response**:
- Sustained grassroots organizing against coal ash
- Legal challenges to permitting
- Health studies documenting impacts

#### San Juan Metropolitan Area
**Environmental Burdens**:
- Traffic-related air pollution (highways through residential areas)
- Urban heat island effect
- Limited green space in low-income neighborhoods
- Waste management facilities in residential areas

**Disparities**:
- Wealthier neighborhoods: Tree-lined streets, parks, coastal access
- Low-income neighborhoods: Limited vegetation, higher temperatures, industrial adjacency

#### Vieques (Former U.S. Navy Bombing Range)
**Environmental Burdens**:
- Unexploded ordinance remaining from 60+ years of military use
- Contamination: Heavy metals, depleted uranium, chemical weapons
- Limited access to former lands for cultural, ecological, economic uses
- Groundwater contamination

**Health Impacts**:
- Cancer rates significantly higher than Puerto Rico average
- Community health registry documents elevated disease rates
- Intergenerational trauma
- Ongoing cleanup inadequate despite Navy departure in 2003

### 1.2 Demographics of Environmental Justice Communities

**Racial and Ethnic Disparities**:
- Afro-descendant communities disproportionately located near polluting facilities
- Historical patterns of environmental racism in facility siting
- Legacy of plantation economy and racial segregation
- Ongoing structural racism in land use and zoning

**Socioeconomic Vulnerability**:
- **Poverty Rate**: 43% overall, 56%+ of children (2025 data)
- **Median Income**: Approximately $24,000 (far below U.S. mainland)
- **Unemployment**: Higher in environmentally burdened communities
- **Education**: Lower educational attainment correlates with environmental burden
- **Housing Quality**: Substandard housing more vulnerable to environmental hazards

**Age Vulnerabilities**:
- **Children**: Highest exposure to air and water pollution during development
- **Elderly**: Limited mobility and resources to escape environmental hazards
- **Chronic illness**: Environmental pollution exacerbating existing conditions

### 1.3 Cumulative Impacts and Synergistic Effects

**Multiple Stressor Framework**:
Environmental justice communities face not single hazards but cumulative, synergistic impacts:

1. **Pollution Burden + Poverty**:
   - Can't afford air filters, water filters
   - Can't relocate from polluted areas
   - Limited healthcare access to treat pollution-related illness

2. **Environmental Hazards + Climate Change**:
   - Coastal pollution + sea-level rise
   - Air pollution + increasing heat
   - Contaminated sites + flooding spreading toxics

3. **Infrastructure Neglect + Disaster Vulnerability**:
   - Aging electrical grid fails first in poor areas
   - Roads in disrepair limit evacuation
   - Inadequate water systems during drought

4. **Colonial Status + Limited Political Power**:
   - No voting representation in U.S. Congress
   - Unequal disaster relief (detailed in Climate Justice document)
   - Inability to set own environmental standards

## 2. Systemic Causes of Environmental Injustice

### 2.1 Colonial Governance Structure

**Federal Control Without Representation**:
- Puerto Rico as "unincorporated territory": Constitutional limbo
- No voting members of Congress to advocate for environmental protection
- Federal agencies treat PR differently than states
- Limited sovereignty over environmental policy

**Historical Roots**:
- U.S. acquired Puerto Rico in 1898 following Spanish-American War
- Treated as military outpost and industrial zone
- Environmental concerns subordinated to military and economic interests
- Pattern of extractive relationship continues

**Current Manifestation (2025)**:
- Environmental regulations often delayed or not fully implemented
- Enforcement weaker than in U.S. states
- Community input less influential in federal decisions
- No state-level environmental agency with full authority

### 2.2 Economic Exploitation and Extractive Industries

**Historical Extraction**:
- Sugarcane plantations (environmental and social devastation)
- Mining operations (copper, nickel) leaving contaminated sites
- Pharmaceutical manufacturing (toxics, water use, waste)
- Military testing (Vieques, other sites)

**Energy Colonialism**:
- Fossil fuel dependence serving corporate profits over community health
- Renewable energy development benefiting outside investors
- Energy poverty while paying highest electricity rates in U.S.
- Community solar blocked by utility regulations

**Current Exploitation**:
- Crypto-mining facilities consuming energy, generating heat/noise
- Proposed mega-projects (natural gas terminals) without community consent
- Cruise ship pollution in San Juan harbor
- Luxury development displacing coastal communities

### 2.3 Institutional Racism and Discrimination

**Facility Siting Patterns**:
- Polluting facilities disproportionately sited in Afro-descendant communities
- Historical zoning reinforcing racial segregation
- "Path of least resistance" targeting communities with limited political power

**Permitting and Enforcement**:
- Permits granted despite community opposition
- Violations under-enforced in environmental justice communities
- Fines too low to deter violations
- Criminal prosecution rare

**Exclusion from Decision-Making**:
- Public participation processes in English (language barrier)
- Meetings held in inaccessible locations or times
- Technical information not translated or explained
- Community input ignored in final decisions

### 2.4 Debt Crisis and Austerity

**PROMESA and the Fiscal Control Board (2016-present)**:
- Federally-imposed oversight prioritizing debt repayment over social needs
- Environmental protection budget cuts
- Staff reductions in Department of Natural Resources (DRNA)
- Monitoring and enforcement capacity diminished

**Impact on Environmental Justice**:
- Less money for parks, conservation, environmental cleanup
- Reduced inspections of polluting facilities
- Deferred maintenance on water and waste systems
- Cuts to environmental health research

**Privatization Pressures**:
- Electric utility privatization (LUMA Energy) prioritizing profit over service
- Water utility privatization proposed
- Public lands sold or leased to developers
- Community access to nature commodified

## 3. Health and Well-being Impacts

### 3.1 Respiratory Illnesses

**Asthma**:
- Puerto Rico has among highest asthma rates in U.S.
- Children in Guayama, Salinas, Peñuelas: Elevated asthma emergency visits
- Coal plant emissions (SO2, particulates) known asthma triggers
- Traffic-related pollution in urban areas

**Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)**:
- Higher rates in communities with industrial air pollution
- Compounded by smoking, indoor air quality
- Limited access to respiratory specialists

### 3.2 Cardiovascular Disease

**Air Pollution Links**:
- Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) increases heart attack and stroke risk
- Long-term exposure to traffic pollution
- Lack of green space for exercise and stress reduction

### 3.3 Cancer

**Vieques Cancer Cluster**:
- Elevated cancer rates documented since military bombing
- Community health registry shows statistical exceedances
- Limited federal response to cleanup or health support

**Other Potential Clusters**:
- Guayama area: Anecdotal reports, limited epidemiological study
- Occupational exposures in pharmaceutical workers
- Need for comprehensive cancer registry with environmental data

### 3.4 Reproductive Health

**Birth Outcomes**:
- Low birth weight associated with air pollution exposure
- Preterm birth rates higher in polluted communities
- Developmental impacts from prenatal exposures

**Fertility Concerns**:
- Endocrine-disrupting chemicals from industrial sources
- Heavy metal exposures affecting reproductive systems

### 3.5 Mental Health and Psychosocial Impacts

**Chronic Stress**:
- Living in polluted environment = constant stressor
- Worry about children's health
- Frustration with lack of political power
- "Environmental grief" over degraded surroundings

**Collective Trauma**:
- Historical trauma from colonialism, slavery, exploitation
- Disaster trauma (hurricanes, earthquakes)
- Environmental trauma (pollution, displacement)
- Intergenerational transmission

### 3.6 Lack of Green Space and Nature Access

**Urban Areas**:
- Low-income neighborhoods have significantly less tree canopy
- Limited access to parks, beaches (privatization, distance)
- Heat island effect increasing temperatures
- Reduced opportunities for nature-based recreation and healing

**Health Implications**:
- Increased heat-related illness
- Limited physical activity opportunities
- Reduced mental health benefits of nature
- Less community cohesion (parks as gathering spaces)

## 4. Access to Clean Water

### 4.1 Water Quality Issues

**Contamination Sources**:
- Agricultural runoff (pesticides, fertilizers)
- Industrial discharges
- Failing septic systems in rural areas
- Aging water infrastructure
- Coal ash pond seepage (Guayama)

**Health Impacts**:
- Gastrointestinal illness from bacterial contamination
- Chronic exposure to chemical contaminants
- Bottled water dependence (expensive, plastic waste)

### 4.2 Water Access Disparities

**Rural Communities**:
- Some areas lack connection to municipal water
- Reliance on wells vulnerable to contamination
- Limited testing of private water sources

**Urban Poor**:
- Water shut-offs for unpaid bills (poverty-driven)
- Aging pipes leaching lead
- Boil water advisories more common in low-income areas

### 4.3 Post-Hurricane Water Crises

**Hurricane Maria (2017) and Ongoing**:
- Water system damage left communities without water for months
- Eight years later (2025), some systems still fragile
- Climate change increasing drought and flood extremes
- Investment inadequate for climate-resilient water infrastructure

## 5. Power Dynamics and Decision-Making

### 5.1 Exclusion from Environmental Governance

**Limited Community Participation**:
- Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) processes often exclude meaningful community input
- **2024 Research Finding**: "Puerto Rico's environmental impact assessment (EIA) process lacks strong procedural justice mechanisms to guarantee meaningful community participation"
- Technical language and English-language documents create barriers
- Short comment periods and inconvenient meeting times

**FEMA's Environmental Assessment Failures**:
- **Post-Hurricane Recovery**: $17 billion from FEMA and $1.9 billion from HUD slated to rebuild same centralized grid and fossil plants
- **Critical Gap**: "FEMA's environmental assessment failed to consider the overburdening of environmental justice communities"
- **Lack of Consent**: "Lacked adequate prior and informed consent from already overburdened communities"

### 5.2 Corporate Power Over Community Needs

**Energy Sector**:
- LUMA Energy (private utility operator) prioritizing profits over reliability
- Rate increases despite poor service
- Renewable energy projects benefiting outside investors
- Community solar blocked by regulatory barriers

**Development**:
- Luxury hotels and resorts given priority over affordable housing
- Coastal privatization limiting public beach access
- Tourism infrastructure straining resources

### 5.3 Federal-Local Power Imbalances

**Territorial Status**:
- Federal laws apply but no voting representation
- Federal agencies make decisions affecting Puerto Rico without input
- Treaty rights not recognized (no indigenous sovereignty framework)

**Fiscal Control Board (PROMESA)**:
- Unelected board with power over Puerto Rico's budget
- Environmental protection subordinated to debt repayment
- No democratic accountability

## 6. Solutions and Community Organizing

### 6.1 Grassroots Environmental Justice Organizations

**Active Groups (as of 2025)**:
- **Comité Diálogo Ambiental**: Environmental advocacy and education
- **Sierra Club Puerto Rico**: Conservation and environmental justice
- **Coalición de Organizaciones Anti-Cenizas**: Fighting coal ash
- **Amig@s del Río Guayanés**: Watershed protection
- **CAMBIO PR**: Climate and environmental justice
- **Vieques en Rescate**: Vieques environmental remediation

**Strategies**:
- Community organizing and education
- Legal challenges to permits and polluters
- Direct action and civil disobedience
- Participatory research (community-based)
- Coalition building across issues

### 6.2 Success Stories

**Coal Plant Closures**:
- AES Puerto Rico (Guayama) coal plant closed 2025 after decades of community pressure
- Aguirre units transitioning away from heavy fuel oil
- Demonstration of people power overcoming corporate interests

**Vieques Navy Ouster (2003)**:
- Sustained civil disobedience ended Navy bombing
- Reclaiming land for community use (partial - much still contaminated)
- Model of successful direct action

**Community Solar Advances**:
- Despite barriers, some community solar projects emerging
- Energy sovereignty demonstrations
- Hurricane resilience through microgrids

### 6.3 Policy Recommendations

#### Federal Level
1. **Full Political Rights**: Statehood or independence with full sovereignty
2. **Equal Treatment**: FEMA, Medicaid, environmental programs on par with states
3. **Superfund Designation**: Vieques and other contaminated sites
4. **Just Transition Funding**: Support coal-impacted communities
5. **Environmental Justice Screening**: Require federal projects to assess EJ impacts
6. **Consent Requirements**: Free, Prior, and Informed Consent for projects

#### Territorial/Local Level
1. **Strengthen DRNA**: Adequate funding and staffing for enforcement
2. **Environmental Justice Law**: Codify protections for overburdened communities
3. **Community Participation**: Mandate early, meaningful participation in all decisions
4. **Cumulative Impact Analysis**: Assess total burden, not individual permits
5. **Green Space Equity**: Target park investment to underserved communities
6. **Water Access as Right**: Prevent shut-offs, ensure affordability

#### Community Level
1. **Capacity Building**: Fund community organizations for technical assistance
2. **Community Science**: Support resident-led monitoring and research
3. **Legal Aid**: Provide legal resources for environmental justice cases
4. **Health Studies**: Comprehensive health registries in impacted communities
5. **Economic Alternatives**: Support green jobs and sustainable development

### 6.4 Integration with Traditional Ecological Knowledge

**Taíno Environmental Wisdom**:
- Reconnecting to indigenous land ethic
- Seven generations thinking in decision-making
- Reciprocity with nature (not extraction)
- Sacred sites as protected areas

**Practical Applications**:
- Traditional agriculture (conuco) as climate-resilient alternative
- Medicinal plants supporting community health
- Seasonal calendars guiding resource use
- Community governance models (bohíos, yucayeques)

## 7. Climate Change Compounding Environmental Injustice

**Interconnection**:
Environmental justice communities are most vulnerable to climate change while having contributed least to it. See separate [Climate Justice Analysis](/data/justice/climate_justice_2025.md) for detailed analysis.

**Key Intersections**:
- Coastal pollution sites threatened by sea-level rise (spreading contamination)
- Urban heat island effect amplified in low-tree-canopy neighborhoods
- Hurricane impacts worst in substandard housing
- Energy insecurity during heat waves (life-threatening)

## 8. Environmental Justice and Public Health Equity

**Social Determinants of Health**:
Environmental quality is a fundamental determinant of health, but unequally distributed:
- Clean air, water, soil: More available to wealthy
- Green space: Segregated by race and class
- Toxic exposure: Concentrated in marginalized communities
- Disaster resilience: Wealth determines recovery

**Health Equity Framework**:
- Environmental justice is health justice
- Cannot achieve health equity without environmental equity
- Require systemic change, not individual behavior
- Address root causes: colonialism, racism, capitalism

## 11. Vision for Environmental Justice Future

**By 2035, Borikén could achieve**:
- No communities overburdened by pollution
- Equitable access to clean air, water, nature
- Community control over local environmental decisions
- Full political rights enabling environmental protection
- Just transition to renewable energy benefiting all
- Restored ecosystems and traditional relationships with land
- Health equity through environmental equity

**This requires**:
- Political will and structural change
- Adequate resources and reparations
- Community power and self-determination
- Decolonization and sovereignty
- Solidarity across movements
- Long-term commitment to justice

## Conclusion

Environmental injustice in Puerto Rico is not accidental but results from centuries of colonialism, racism, and exploitation. Communities in Guayama, Salinas, Peñuelas, Vieques, and throughout the island have borne disproportionate environmental burdens while having limited power to protect themselves.

However, these same communities have demonstrated remarkable resilience, organizing power, and vision for a just future. From closing coal plants to resisting militarization to building renewable energy alternatives, environmental justice movements in Borikén are showing the way forward.

**The path to environmental justice requires recognizing that ecology cannot be separated from justice—protecting Borikén's biodiversity means protecting its people, especially those most impacted by environmental harm.**
