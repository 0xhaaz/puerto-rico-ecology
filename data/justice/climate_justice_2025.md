# Climate Justice in Borikén (Puerto Rico) - 2025 Analysis

## Executive Summary

Climate justice in Puerto Rico reveals stark inequities: the island has contributed minimally to global climate change yet faces disproportionate impacts, with vulnerable communities—particularly low-income residents, the elderly, and rural populations—bearing the heaviest burdens. Eight years after Hurricane Maria (2017), recovery disparities persist, with environmental justice communities experiencing the slowest restoration of essential services. As of 2025, only 12% of Puerto Rico's energy comes from renewable sources, far below the 40% goal set for 2025, while residents pay the highest electricity rates in the United States.

**Key Finding**: Climate change is not just an environmental crisis but a justice crisis, amplifying existing inequities rooted in colonial governance, poverty, and systemic neglect.

## 1. Disproportionate Climate Impacts on Vulnerable Communities

### 1.1 Hurricane Recovery Inequities

**Hurricane Maria (September 20, 2017) - Eight Years Later (2025)**

#### Immediate Impacts
- **Death toll**: Estimated near 5,000 (many indirect deaths from lack of power, medical care, water)
- **Population displacement**: At least 130,000 Puerto Ricans (nearly 5% of population) forced to migrate
- **Power outage**: Island-wide blackout lasting months in many areas
- **Water system failure**: Widespread, prolonged lack of potable water
- **Infrastructure damage**: $90+ billion in damages

#### Recovery Disparities (2017-2025)

**Environmental Justice Analysis**:
Research shows **inequitable hurricane recovery** in Puerto Rico, with:
- **Central rural communities** last to have power restored
- **Black communities** experiencing longest power outages
- **Low-income areas** slowest to receive federal aid
- **Urban-adjacent areas** showing hindered recovery even in natural systems (e.g., mangroves)

**Federal Response Inequity**:
- Puerto Rico received **significantly less FEMA aid** in days after Hurricane Maria than Texas and Florida, which had also been impacted by major hurricanes in 2017
- **Unequal treatment**: Federal policies treat Puerto Rico differently than the 50 states, putting Puerto Ricans' health and recovery at disadvantage
- **Three key policy differences**:
  1. Unequal disaster response from FEMA
  2. Lack of parity in Medicaid funding
  3. Puerto Rico's limited political power (no voting representation in Congress)

#### Ongoing Challenges (2025)
- **Grid fragility**: Electric system remains vulnerable to storms
- **Housing**: Many homes still not fully repaired or rebuilt
- **Mental health**: Chronic trauma from disaster and slow recovery
- **Out-migration**: Continued population loss affecting community cohesion

### 1.2 Heat Vulnerability

**Urban Heat Island Effect**:
- Low-income neighborhoods have **significantly less tree canopy**
- Concrete and asphalt increase local temperatures by 5-10°F
- Lack of green space reduces cooling opportunities

**Air Conditioning Access**:
- **Energy poverty** (43% poverty rate) limits AC access and use
- Highest electricity rates in U.S. ($0.30+/kWh) make cooling unaffordable
- Power outages during heat waves create life-threatening situations
- Elderly residents particularly vulnerable

**Heat-Related Health Impacts**:
- Increased hospitalizations during heat waves
- Occupational heat stress for outdoor workers
- Exacerbation of chronic conditions (cardiovascular, respiratory)
- Disproportionate impacts on homeless population

### 1.3 Flooding and Sea-Level Rise

**Coastal Flooding**:
- Many low-income communities located in flood-prone coastal areas
- Lack of resources for flood-proofing homes
- Limited evacuation transportation options
- Repeated flooding causing cumulative damage

**Sea-Level Rise Projections**:
- 1-3 feet by 2100 (moderate scenarios)
- Threatens coastal communities, infrastructure, ecosystems
- Saltwater intrusion into freshwater aquifers
- Erosion of beaches and coastal habitats

**Inland Flooding**:
- Deforested areas experiencing increased runoff
- Inadequate stormwater infrastructure
- Low-lying areas with poor drainage
- Flood insurance unaffordable for many residents

### 1.4 Drought and Water Insecurity

**Increasing Drought**:
- Climate models project longer, more severe droughts
- Extended dry seasons (February-April becoming drier)
- Water restrictions disproportionately affect rural areas

**Water Infrastructure Fragility**:
- Aging pipes leaking significant water (>50% in some areas)
- Rural areas dependent on wells vulnerable to drought
- Water shut-offs for unpaid bills affecting poor households
- Limited investment in climate-resilient water systems

### 1.5 Food System Vulnerability

**Climate + Import Dependence**:
- 85% food import dependence makes PR vulnerable to shipping disruptions
- Climate change affecting maritime routes and food prices
- Local agriculture stressed by hurricanes, drought, heat
- Food insecurity rising with climate impacts

**Agricultural Impacts**:
- Crop losses from hurricanes and drought
- Soil degradation from extreme weather
- Pest and disease changes with warming
- Small farmers lacking resources for adaptation

## 2. Historical Responsibility and Colonial Context

### 2.1 Minimal Contribution to Climate Change

**Low Per-Capita Emissions**:
- Puerto Rico's greenhouse gas emissions far below U.S. mainland average
- Historical emissions negligible compared to industrialized nations
- Most emissions from imported fossil fuels for electricity
- Colonial economy never underwent full industrialization

**Imposed Energy System**:
- Fossil fuel dependence imposed through colonial economic structure
- No control over energy policy until recent decades
- Renewable energy development limited by utility monopoly
- Infrastructure investments prioritized fossil fuels

### 2.2 Colonial Governance and Climate Vulnerability

**Limited Self-Determination**:
- No voting representation in U.S. Congress
- Federal climate policy decided without Puerto Rican input
- Cannot enter international climate agreements independently
- Fiscal Control Board (PROMESA) prioritizes debt over climate adaptation

**Unequal Federal Treatment**:
- **FEMA**: Less disaster aid, slower response than states
- **Medicaid**: Cap on funding (unlike states with unlimited matching)
- **Climate adaptation funding**: Lower per-capita allocation
- **Environmental regulations**: Sometimes not fully implemented

**Debt Crisis Impact**:
- $70+ billion debt limiting climate adaptation investment
- Austerity measures cutting environmental and social services
- Infrastructure maintenance deferred
- Public sector capacity reduced

### 2.3 Historical Extraction and Environmental Degradation

**Colonial Economic Patterns**:
- Deforestation for sugarcane plantations
- Soil erosion reducing agricultural resilience
- Loss of traditional agroecological systems
- Concentration of pollution in marginalized communities

**Military Impacts**:
- Vieques bombing range contamination
- Loss of land for community resilience
- Environmental damage reducing adaptive capacity
- Unexploded ordinance limiting land use

## 3. Climate Adaptation Justice

### 3.1 Who Benefits from Adaptation Investments?

**Investment Disparities**:
Research and monitoring show that climate adaptation investments are not reaching those most vulnerable:

- **Tourism sector**: Receives significant investment (coastal hotels, resorts)
- **Wealthy neighborhoods**: Tree planting, green infrastructure
- **Federal facilities**: Upgraded to climate resilience
- **Low-income communities**: Minimal adaptation investment

**Managed Retreat vs. Community Displacement**:
- Climate adaptation plans sometimes prioritize "managed retreat"
- Risk of displacing communities from ancestral lands
- Buyout programs may not provide adequate compensation
- Cultural and community ties severed by forced relocation
- Gentrification following climate investments ("climate gentrification")

**Green Infrastructure Access**:
- Parks and green spaces provide climate resilience
- Low-income neighborhoods have least green infrastructure
- Investments often favor wealthier areas
- Lack of participatory planning process

### 3.2 Energy System Inequity and Climate Adaptation

**Centralized Grid Vulnerability**:
- Hurricane Maria demonstrated fragility of centralized fossil fuel system
- **$17 billion FEMA funding** + **$1.9 billion HUD** slated to **rebuild same centralized grid**
- Perpetuates fossil fuel dependence and vulnerability
- Community input excluded from recovery planning

**Critical Gap in FEMA Planning**:
- FEMA's environmental assessment **"failed to consider the overburdening of environmental justice communities"**
- **Lacked adequate prior and informed consent** from overburdened communities
- Recovery plan prioritizes utility company profits over community resilience

**Renewable Energy Access Barriers**:
- Community solar blocked by utility regulations
- Rooftop solar affordable mainly for wealthy households
- **Unjust divides** between those who can afford solar+storage and those dependent on fragile grid
- Permitting process favors large corporate projects over community-scale

**Energy Poverty and Climate**:
- **43% poverty rate**, including **56%+ of children**
- Highest electricity rates in U.S. while service is unreliable
- Cannot afford backup generators or battery storage
- Power outages during heat waves and hurricanes life-threatening
- **Only 12% renewable energy** as of 2024 (far below 40% goal for 2025)

### 3.3 Housing and Built Environment

**Substandard Housing Vulnerability**:
- Many low-income homes not built to hurricane standards
- Lack of resources for retrofitting or rebuilding
- Informal housing particularly vulnerable
- Historic structures lacking protection

**Land Tenure Insecurity**:
- Unclear title in some rural areas limiting recovery aid
- Risk of losing land after climate disasters
- Lack of flood insurance (unaffordable)
- Difficulty accessing federal disaster loans

## 4. Just Transition and Renewable Energy

### 4.1 Community Energy Sovereignty

**Vision for Justice**:
A just energy transition means:
- **Community ownership** of renewable energy
- **Equitable access** to clean, affordable, reliable power
- **Local decision-making** over energy systems
- **Job creation** in renewable energy for all communities
- **Resilience** through distributed generation and microgrids

**Barriers to Just Transition**:
- **LUMA Energy** (private utility operator) prioritizing profits over community needs
- Regulatory framework favoring large corporate projects
- Lack of financing for community-scale projects
- Limited technical assistance for community organizations
- Fossil fuel interests resisting transition

**Successful Models**:
Despite barriers, some community energy projects emerging:
- Casa Pueblo (Adjuntas): Solar microgrid serving community
- Community solar gardens (limited but growing)
- Cooperative models being explored
- Post-Maria, many installed solar+battery systems

### 4.2 Green Jobs and Economic Opportunity

**Job Creation Potential**:
- Renewable energy installation and maintenance
- Energy efficiency retrofitting
- Agroecology and sustainable agriculture
- Ecosystem restoration (mangroves, forests)
- Climate adaptation infrastructure

**Just Transition Principles**:
- **Prioritize hiring** from affected communities
- **Living wages** and union protections
- **Training programs** accessible to all
- **Worker transition support** from fossil fuel industries
- **Local ownership** of renewable energy projects

**Current Reality**:
- Renewable energy jobs often going to outside contractors
- Local communities not benefiting from green economy
- Training programs insufficient
- Lack of coordinated just transition strategy

## 5. Traditional Resilience and Indigenous Knowledge

### 5.1 Taíno Traditional Climate Adaptation

**Indigenous Climate Wisdom**:
- **Hurricane preparedness**: Seasonal observations, early warning signs
- **Conuco agriculture**: Polyculture systems resilient to drought and storms
- **Forest management**: Maintaining ecosystem services for resilience
- **Coastal adaptation**: Traditional knowledge of tides, storms, fish behavior
- **Community cooperation**: Collective response to disasters

**Application to Modern Climate Adaptation**:
- Traditional agriculture more resilient than monocultures
- Indigenous seasonal calendars guide planting and harvesting
- Community-based response more effective than top-down
- Reciprocal relationship with nature reduces vulnerability

### 5.2 Living in Harmony with Climate

**Seven Generations Thinking**:
- Taíno principle of considering impacts seven generations ahead
- Contrasts with short-term profit-driven development
- Climate justice requires long-term thinking
- Responsibility to future generations

**Sacred Relationship with Land**:
- Climate change as spiritual crisis, not just technical problem
- Healing relationship with nature as climate solution
- Protecting sacred sites provides climate resilience
- Community care and mutual aid as adaptation strategy

## 7. Climate Justice Policy Recommendations

### 7.1 Federal Level

**Political Rights and Self-Determination**:
1. **Full political rights**: End colonial status (statehood or independence)
2. **Climate policy participation**: Voice in federal climate legislation
3. **Equal federal treatment**: Same FEMA, disaster aid, climate funding as states
4. **Debt relief**: Cancel or restructure debt to enable climate investment

**Climate Adaptation Funding**:
1. **Adequate appropriations**: Based on vulnerability, not political power
2. **Community-controlled funding**: Direct resources to frontline communities
3. **Just recovery**: Prioritize most vulnerable in disaster recovery
4. **Renewable energy investment**: Federal support for community solar and microgrids

**Environmental Justice Requirements**:
1. **Climate EJ screening**: All federal actions assessed for justice impacts
2. **Free, Prior, Informed Consent**: Community approval for projects
3. **Cumulative impact analysis**: Consider total climate vulnerability
4. **Benefits to frontline communities**: 40% of climate funding (Justice40 Initiative)

### 7.2 Territorial/Local Level

**Energy Transformation**:
1. **100% renewable energy by 2050** (current law, need implementation)
2. **Community energy sovereignty**: Support local ownership
3. **End fossil fuel subsidies**: Redirect to renewables
4. **Microgrid development**: Hurricane-resilient distributed generation
5. **Affordable rates**: Energy as human right, not commodity

**Climate Adaptation Planning**:
1. **Community-led planning**: Participatory processes with frontline communities
2. **Nature-based solutions**: Mangrove restoration, reforestation, agroecology
3. **Retreat with justice**: If relocation necessary, ensure fairness and community input
4. **Green infrastructure equity**: Target investments to most vulnerable areas
5. **Housing climate resilience**: Retrofit and upgrade low-income housing

**Build Resilience Capacity**:
1. **Strengthen emergency management**: Community-based, culturally appropriate
2. **Climate education**: Integrate into schools and communities
3. **Health system adaptation**: Prepare for climate health impacts
4. **Water security**: Climate-resilient water infrastructure
5. **Food sovereignty**: Support local, sustainable agriculture

### 7.3 Community Level

**Grassroots Organizing**:
1. **Build community power**: Organize for decision-making authority
2. **Mutual aid networks**: Collective disaster preparedness and response
3. **Alternative institutions**: Community-owned energy, food systems
4. **Climate literacy**: Popular education on climate and justice
5. **Intergenerational exchange**: Connect elders' knowledge with youth energy

**Solidarity and Coalition Building**:
1. **Link climate to other justice struggles**: Housing, health, labor, anti-colonialism
2. **Regional cooperation**: Caribbean climate justice network
3. **Global solidarity**: Connect to international climate justice movement
4. **Indigenous leadership**: Center Taíno and indigenous perspectives
5. **Culture and art**: Use culture to build movement and vision

## Conclusion

Climate justice in Puerto Rico illuminates the deep connections between colonialism, racism, poverty, and climate vulnerability. Eight years after Hurricane Maria, inequities in recovery persist, demonstrating that climate disasters amplify existing injustices. As of 2025, Puerto Rico stands at a crossroads: continue on a path of fossil fuel dependence and centralized power that leaves communities vulnerable, or transform toward community-led, renewable, resilient systems that serve people and planet.

**Climate justice requires recognizing that those who contributed least to climate change—Puerto Ricans, indigenous peoples, communities of color, low-income communities—are suffering most. True climate action must address these injustices, not perpetuate them.**

The vision of climate justice in Puerto Rico is not just about surviving climate change, but thriving through transformation—toward energy sovereignty, food sovereignty, community power, ecological restoration, and ultimately, full self-determination for the people of Borikén.
