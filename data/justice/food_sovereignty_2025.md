# Food Sovereignty and Justice in Borikén (Puerto Rico) - 2025 Analysis

## Executive Summary

Puerto Rico faces a critical food sovereignty crisis: the island produces only 10-15% of the food it consumes, importing 85% from the United States mainland, primarily through the port of Jacksonville, Florida. This extreme dependence creates profound vulnerability to climate change, economic shocks, and supply chain disruptions while undermining cultural identity, public health, and environmental sustainability. However, a growing agroecology movement is demonstrating that another path is possible—experts estimate that Puerto Rico could produce two-thirds of its food needs if 600,000 acres of reserved agricultural land were developed for agroecological farms.

**Key Finding**: Food sovereignty is not just about agriculture—it's about self-determination, decolonization, cultural preservation, environmental justice, and the right of communities to control their own food systems.

## 1. The Food Dependency Crisis

### 1.1 Import Dependence and Vulnerability

**Current Status (2025)**:
- **85% food import dependence** (some sources indicate 85-90%)
- **Only 10-15% of food** produced locally
- **Primary shipping route**: Port of Jacksonville, Florida to Puerto Rico
- **Maritime dependency**: Nearly all food arrives by ship

**Historical Context**:
- Early 20th century: Puerto Rico produced much of its own food
- U.S. colonial period: Shift to export crops (sugar, coffee, tobacco)
- Post-WWII: Industrialization and abandonment of agriculture
- 1970s-present: Accelerating food import dependence
- Lost agricultural knowledge and infrastructure

### 1.2 Vulnerabilities Created by Dependence

**Climate Change Vulnerability**:
- **Hurricanes disrupting shipping**: Hurricane Maria left stores empty for weeks
- **Sea-level rise threatening ports**: Infrastructure at risk
- **Climate affecting global food production**: Price spikes and shortages
- **Maritime route vulnerability**: Storms, fuel prices, geopolitical disruptions

**Economic Vulnerability**:
- **Food prices tied to shipping costs**: Rising fuel = rising food prices
- **Jones Act**: Requiring U.S.-flagged ships increases costs 20-30%
- **Currency dependence**: U.S. dollar fluctuations affect purchasing power
- **Global market volatility**: Puerto Rico cannot control food prices

**Supply Chain Disruptions**:
- **COVID-19 pandemic**: Exposed fragility of food system
- **Ship delays**: Any maritime problem causes shortages
- **Single point of failure**: Dependence on one port, one shipping route
- **Just-in-time delivery**: Minimal food reserves on island

**National Security and Self-Determination**:
- **Food as weapon**: Dependent population can be controlled through food access
- **Colonial control**: U.S. controls Puerto Rico's food supply
- **Loss of sovereignty**: Cannot independently feed population
- **Political leverage**: Food dependence limits political options

## 2. Loss of Agricultural Land and Capacity

### 2.1 Land Use Changes

**Agricultural Land Decline**:
- **600,000 acres** of designated agricultural land largely unused
- **Historical peak**: Over 60% of land in agriculture (early 1900s)
- **Current**: Less than 6% of land actively farmed
- **Development pressure**: Conversion to urban, industrial, commercial uses
- **Abandonment**: Farms reverting to secondary forest or invasive species

**Soil Degradation**:
- **Erosion**: Historical sugarcane monocultures depleted soils
- **Compaction**: Decades of heavy equipment use
- **Chemical contamination**: Legacy pesticide and fertilizer residues
- **Loss of organic matter**: Intensive farming depleted soil health
- **Restoration needed**: Years of work to rebuild soil fertility

### 2.2 Loss of Agricultural Knowledge and Infrastructure

**Knowledge Loss**:
- **Generational disconnect**: Youth not learning farming
- **Elder farmers aging**: Traditional knowledge disappearing
- **Educational system**: Agriculture not taught in schools
- **Brain drain**: Trained agronomists leaving island
- **Taíno agricultural wisdom**: Largely lost or marginalized

**Infrastructure Collapse**:
- **Processing facilities**: Closed or outdated
- **Distribution networks**: Geared toward imports, not local food
- **Storage**: Limited capacity for local crops
- **Equipment**: Lack of appropriate small-scale machinery
- **Irrigation**: Systems not maintained

**Seed Sovereignty Crisis**:
- **Hybrid and GMO dominance**: Dependence on purchased seeds
- **Loss of traditional varieties**: Locally-adapted landraces disappearing
- **Intellectual property**: Patents limiting farmer seed saving
- **Import dependence**: Even seeds come from off-island

## 3. Health and Nutritional Impacts

### 3.1 Diet-Related Health Crisis

**Chronic Disease Epidemic**:
- **Diabetes**: Over 13% of adults (one of highest rates in U.S.)
- **Obesity**: Over 30% of adults, 15-20% of children
- **Cardiovascular disease**: Leading cause of death
- **Hypertension**: Affecting large percentage of population
- **Cancer**: Increasing rates

**Dietary Factors**:
- **Ultra-processed foods**: Cheap, shelf-stable imports dominate
- **High sodium, sugar, fat**: Processed foods engineered for shelf life, not health
- **Low fresh produce**: Expensive and limited availability
- **Traditional diets abandoned**: Shift from plant-based traditional foods to processed imports
- **Food deserts**: Many communities lack access to fresh, healthy food

**Economic Barriers to Healthy Food**:
- **43% poverty rate**: Cannot afford fresh, healthy food
- **Fresh produce expensive**: Imported fruits and vegetables costly
- **Processed cheaper**: Economic incentive to choose unhealthy
- **Food assistance programs**: Often used for processed foods due to cost and availability

### 3.2 Cultural and Spiritual Dimensions of Food

**Loss of Food Culture**:
- **Traditional recipes forgotten**: Younger generations not learning
- **Ingredients unavailable**: Traditional crops not cultivated
- **Fast food dominance**: Cultural homogenization
- **Disconnect from land**: Food as commodity, not connection to place
- **Family and community bonds**: Traditional food preparation as social glue weakened

**Traditional Foods and Health**:
- **Yuca (cassava)**: Nutrient-dense staple
- **Ñame (yam)**: Traditional carbohydrate source
- **Plátano (plantain)**: Versatile, nutritious
- **Legumes**: Beans, gandules (pigeon peas) - protein and fiber
- **Viandas**: Root vegetables central to Taíno and Puerto Rican cuisine
- **Tropical fruits**: Mangoes, papayas, guavas, soursop
- **Leafy greens**: Traditional pot herbs
- **Fresh fish**: Sustainable protein source

**Food as Medicine**:
- **Traditional medicinal plants**: Often grown alongside food
- **Healing foods**: Specific foods for specific ailments
- **Prevention through diet**: Traditional wisdom on health maintenance
- **Holistic health**: Food as part of integrated wellness system

## 4. The Agroecology Movement: Rebuilding Food Sovereignty

### 4.1 What is Agroecology?

**Definition**:
Agroecology is the application of ecological principles to agriculture, creating sustainable food systems that:
- Work with nature, not against it
- Reduce external inputs (purchased seeds, synthetic chemicals)
- Build soil health and biodiversity
- Integrate traditional and scientific knowledge
- Prioritize local food security over export cash crops
- Promote farmer autonomy and community control

**Agroecology vs. Industrial Agriculture**:
| Agroecology | Industrial Agriculture |
|-------------|------------------------|
| Polyculture (multiple crops) | Monoculture (single crop) |
| Ecological pest management | Chemical pesticides |
| Organic fertilizers, composting | Synthetic fertilizers |
| Seed saving, local varieties | Purchased hybrid/GMO seeds |
| Small-scale, diverse | Large-scale, specialized |
| Community-oriented | Corporate-controlled |
| Resilient to shocks | Vulnerable to disruptions |

### 4.2 Agroecology as Survival Strategy

**Quote from Experts**:
> "Agroecology is the only way that Caribbean islands have survived, and the only way they can survive."

**Resilience to Climate Change**:
- **Polycultures** more resistant to hurricanes than monocultures
- **Diverse crops** spread risk across multiple species
- **Soil health** increases water retention during drought
- **Local varieties** adapted to Puerto Rico's climate
- **Ecological diversity** provides natural pest control

**Food Security Benefits**:
- **Year-round production** possible in tropical climate
- **Diverse outputs** providing nutrition variety
- **Multiple harvests** from perennial and annual crops
- **Reduced dependence** on external inputs and imports
- **Community food reserves** from local production

### 4.3 Agroecology in Practice: Success Stories (2025)

**Finca El Vapor (San Lorenzo)**:
- Part of Land Trust of Institute for Agroecology (IALA)
- Demonstration of agroecological methods
- Training new generation of farmers
- Proof that alternative model is possible

**Finca Güakiá (San Lorenzo)**:
- Integrated into IALA Land Trust
- Collective land tenure model
- Ecological farming practices
- Community education and engagement

**Las Perdices Farm (Ciales)**:
- **31 acres** donated May 2023
- Located in karst region (ecologically important)
- Designated as **inaugural Agroecological Commons**
- Committed to long-term agroecology for food sovereignty
- Model for future commons

**Casa Pueblo (Adjuntas)**:
- Community organization integrating sustainable agriculture with renewable energy
- Agroforestry and shade-grown coffee
- Community resilience hub
- Demonstration of integrated community sustainability

**Growing Agroecology Movement**:
- **New generation of farmers** trained in agroecological methods
- **Organizations**: IALA, Organización Boricuá, local cooperatives
- **Practices combine**: Sustainable production, environmental respect, collective tenure
- **Spreading throughout archipelago**: Knowledge sharing and replication

### 4.4 Potential for Food Self-Sufficiency

**Expert Assessment**:
> "The island could yield about **two-thirds of all the food it needs** for all its residents if it redeveloped its **600,000 acres of reserved agricultural land** for agroecological farms."

**What This Means**:
- From 10-15% to 65%+ food self-sufficiency possible
- Requires shift to agroecological methods (not industrial monocultures)
- Land is available but needs restoration and farmer access
- Knowledge exists but needs to be spread and supported
- Policy support essential for transformation

**Barriers to Scaling Up**:
- **Land access**: Much agricultural land owned by absentee landlords or corporations
- **Credit and financing**: Banks don't understand agroecology
- **Technical assistance**: Limited agroecology extension services
- **Markets**: Distribution infrastructure geared toward imports
- **Policy**: Government support historically favored industrial agriculture
- **Cultural**: Perception that farming is low-status work

## 5. Food Sovereignty as Decolonization

### 5.1 La Vía Campesina Definition

**Food Sovereignty** (per La Vía Campesina, international peasant movement):
> "The right of peoples to healthy and culturally appropriate food produced through ecologically sound and sustainable methods, and their right to define their own food and agriculture systems."

**Key Principles**:
1. **Food as human right**, not commodity
2. **Community control** over food systems
3. **Ecological sustainability**
4. **Cultural appropriateness**
5. **Fair conditions** for food producers
6. **Protection from corporate exploitation**

### 5.2 Restructuring the Agro-Food System

**What Food Sovereignty Requires**:
- **Land reform**: Access to land for farmers, not corporations
- **Fair prices**: For farmers and affordable for consumers
- **Democratic governance**: Communities deciding food policies
- **Protection of farmers**: From corporate exploitation and land grabbing
- **Support for small farmers**: Not just industrial agriculture
- **Seed sovereignty**: Right to save, use, exchange seeds
- **Local markets**: Direct farmer-consumer relationships
- **Living wages**: Dignified livelihoods for farmers and farm workers

**Colonial vs. Sovereign Food Systems**:
| Colonial System (Current) | Sovereign System (Goal) |
|---------------------------|------------------------|
| Import-dependent | Locally-produced |
| Corporate-controlled | Community-controlled |
| Monoculture cash crops | Diverse food crops |
| Chemical-intensive | Agroecological |
| Distant production | Local food webs |
| Profit-driven | Need-driven |
| Cultural homogenization | Cultural preservation |
| Environmental degradation | Ecological regeneration |

### 5.3 Taíno Food Sovereignty: Conuco System

**Traditional Agricultural System**:
The **conuco** was the Taíno agricultural system—a model of sustainable, diverse food production:

**Conuco Characteristics**:
- **Polyculture**: Multiple crops in same space
- **Mounds (montones)**: Raised planting beds for drainage and soil warmth
- **Crop combinations**: The "three sisters" of Americas (maize, beans, squash) plus yuca, sweet potato, peppers, tropical fruits
- **Perennial crops**: Trees providing continuous harvest
- **Minimal tillage**: Preserving soil structure
- **Organic matter**: Composting and mulching
- **Water management**: Swales and berms directing rainfall
- **Biodiversity**: Attracting beneficial insects and wildlife

**Crops of the Conuco**:
- **Yuca (cassava)**: Primary staple, diverse varieties
- **Batata (sweet potato)**: Important carbohydrate
- **Maíz (corn)**: Grain and vegetable
- **Habichuelas (beans)**: Protein and nitrogen-fixing
- **Ajíes (peppers)**: Seasoning and medicine
- **Calabaza (squash)**: Vegetable and seeds
- **Piña (pineapple)**: Fruit native to Americas
- **Many others**: Diverse, adapted varieties

**Ecological Wisdom**:
- **Mimics forest**: Multiple layers of vegetation
- **Self-sustaining**: Minimal external inputs needed
- **Resilient**: Diversity protects against crop failures
- **Productive**: Yields more per acre than monoculture
- **Sustainable**: Can be maintained indefinitely

**Reclaiming Conuco**:
Modern agroecology movement **rediscovering** and **adapting** conuco principles:
- Small farms using polyculture methods
- Incorporating Taíno crop varieties
- Honoring indigenous agricultural knowledge
- Teaching new generations

## 6. Economic Justice and Food Systems

### 6.1 Corporate Control vs. Community Ownership

**Current Corporate Control**:
- **Supermarket chains**: Mostly U.S. corporations
- **Food processing**: Off-island companies
- **Seed companies**: Multinational corporations
- **Distribution**: Corporate supply chains
- **Profits leave island**: Extractive relationship

**Alternative Economic Models**:
- **Farmers cooperatives**: Collective processing and marketing
- **Community Supported Agriculture (CSA)**: Direct farmer-consumer relationship
- **Food hubs**: Aggregating and distributing local food
- **Community gardens**: Direct production by residents
- **Farmers markets**: Face-to-face exchange
- **Solidarity economy**: Prioritizing community wellbeing over profit

### 6.2 Fair Prices and Living Wages

**Farmer Livelihoods**:
- **Current**: Small farmers struggle to survive economically
- **Industrial model**: "Get big or get out" pushes small farmers off land
- **Fair prices needed**: Consumers pay true cost, farmers receive living wage
- **Value-added processing**: Farmers capturing more value
- **Collective bargaining**: Cooperatives improving farmer bargaining power

**Food Worker Justice**:
- **Farm workers**: Often exploited, low wages, poor conditions
- **Processing workers**: Need safe conditions, fair wages
- **Restaurant workers**: Part of food system, often low-paid
- **Food sovereignty includes**: Justice for all food system workers

### 6.3 Food Access and Affordability

**Current Inequality**:
- **Wealthy**: Access to organic, local, imported specialty foods
- **Poor**: Limited to ultra-processed, unhealthy options
- **Geographic**: Rural and low-income urban areas are food deserts

**Food Justice Solutions**:
- **Community gardens**: Free or low-cost food access
- **Sliding scale CSAs**: Affordable for all income levels
- **Food assistance**: Supporting local food, not just industrial
- **Community kitchens**: Collective cooking reducing costs
- **Gleaning programs**: Rescuing unharvested food for distribution

## 7. Environmental Connections

### 7.1 Agriculture and Biodiversity

**Industrial Agriculture Threats**:
- **Monocultures**: Reducing habitat diversity
- **Pesticides**: Harming pollinators, soil life, water quality
- **Deforestation**: Clearing forests for industrial farms
- **Invasive species**: Some crops become invasive (e.g., African grasses)

**Agroecology Benefits**:
- **Habitat creation**: Diverse farms support wildlife
- **Pollinator support**: Flowers and diverse plantings
- **Soil biodiversity**: Healthy soils teeming with life
- **Corridors**: Farms connecting forest patches
- **Integrated ecosystems**: Agriculture within larger landscape

### 7.2 Agriculture and Climate

**Industrial Agriculture Contributions to Climate Change**:
- **Synthetic fertilizers**: High energy inputs, nitrous oxide emissions
- **Pesticide production**: Energy-intensive
- **Long-distance transport**: Food miles and carbon footprint
- **Soil degradation**: Releases stored carbon

**Agroecology Climate Solutions**:
- **Carbon sequestration**: Building soil organic matter
- **Reduced emissions**: Minimal external inputs
- **Local food**: Reduced transportation
- **Climate resilience**: Diverse systems adapting to change
- **Perennial crops**: Continuous soil cover and carbon storage

## 8. Policy and Structural Changes Needed

### 8.1 Land Reform

**Essential Actions**:
1. **Access to land**: Redistribute idle agricultural land to farmers
2. **Secure tenure**: Protect farmers from eviction and land speculation
3. **Commons model**: Community land trusts for permanent affordability
4. **End land grabbing**: Prevent corporate and investor land acquisition
5. **Support young farmers**: Help new generation access land

### 8.2 Agricultural Support

**Needed Policies**:
1. **Agroecology extension**: Technical assistance for ecological farming
2. **Farmer training**: Programs in agroecological methods
3. **Financing**: Credit for small farmers, agroecology projects
4. **Research**: Public investment in agroecology research
5. **Seed systems**: Support for local seed saving and exchange
6. **Processing infrastructure**: Community-scale facilities

### 8.3 Market Development

**Policy Interventions**:
1. **Local food procurement**: Government, schools, hospitals buying local
2. **Farmers markets**: Support and incentives
3. **Food hubs**: Infrastructure for aggregation and distribution
4. **Value-added processing**: Support for farm-based enterprises
5. **Fair pricing**: Policies ensuring farmers receive fair prices

### 8.4 Education and Culture

**Cultural Transformation**:
1. **School curricula**: Agriculture and food sovereignty education
2. **School gardens**: Hands-on learning and school food source
3. **Culinary programs**: Traditional foods and cooking
4. **Media campaigns**: Valuing local food and farmers
5. **Cultural events**: Celebrating food heritage

## 9. COVID-19 Pandemic and Food System Resilience

**Pandemic Revealed Fragility**:
- **Supply chain disruptions**: Empty shelves in supermarkets
- **Import dependence vulnerability**: Shipping delays caused shortages
- **Economic crisis**: Increased food insecurity
- **Health crisis**: Underlying health conditions from poor diet exacerbated COVID impacts

**Pandemic Response**:
- **Community gardens expanded**: People growing own food
- **Mutual aid networks**: Communities sharing food
- **Local food systems**: Proved more resilient than global supply chains
- **Renewed interest**: More people recognizing importance of food sovereignty

**Lessons Learned**:
- **Resilience requires local production**
- **Community cooperation essential**
- **Health and food security connected**
- **Dependence is dangerous**

- **Thriving small farmers**: Living wages, secure land tenure
- **Agroecological farms**: 600,000 acres in diverse, ecological production
- **Healthy population**: Diet-related diseases declining
- **Cultural revival**: Traditional foods, recipes, knowledge reclaimed
- **Ecological restoration**: Agriculture enhancing biodiversity
- **Community food systems**: Direct relationships, local markets
- **Food security**: Everyone has access to healthy, culturally appropriate food
- **Seed sovereignty**: Locally-adapted varieties saved and shared
- **Next generation**: Youth learning farming, cooking, food traditions
- **Climate resilience**: Food system adapted to climate change
- **Economic justice**: Fair prices for farmers, affordable for all
- **Self-determination**: Puerto Rico controlling its own food system

**Getting there requires**:
- **Political will**: Government support for transformation
- **Land reform**: Access for farmers
- **Investment**: Financing food sovereignty infrastructure
- **Education**: Training farmers and consumers
- **Policy change**: Laws supporting agroecology and local food

## Conclusion

Food sovereignty in Puerto Rico is fundamentally about self-determination and decolonization. The current system—importing 85% of food through corporate-controlled supply chains—is a colonial relationship that undermines health, culture, environment, and resilience. However, the agroecology movement is demonstrating that another path is possible: Puerto Rico could produce two-thirds of its food through ecological farming on available agricultural land.

**Food sovereignty is not just about agriculture. It's about reclaiming control over a fundamental aspect of life, reconnecting to land and culture, building community resilience, protecting health, and healing the environment. It's about the right to feed yourself, your family, your community—on your own terms, in your own place, in harmony with nature.**

The vision of food sovereignty honors the Taíno conuco, integrates modern agroecology, centers community control, ensures justice for farmers and workers, and provides healthy, culturally appropriate food for all. It's a vision of Borikén feeding itself, sustainably and justly, for generations to come.
