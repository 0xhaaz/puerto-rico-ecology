# Economic Justice and Ecology in Borikén (Puerto Rico) - 2025 Analysis

## Executive Summary

Economic inequality in Puerto Rico is among the most severe in the United States, with a GINI index of .545 (compared to .482 US average), a 43% poverty rate, and extreme wealth concentration. This economic injustice is deeply intertwined with ecology: natural resources generate billions in tourism revenue ($2B+ annually from coral reefs alone), yet wealth flows to external corporations and the top 1% while local communities bear environmental costs. Act 60 tax incentives attract wealthy mainlanders, driving gentrification and displacement, while 1.4 million Puerto Ricans survive on less than $22,000 annually. True ecological conservation requires economic justice—ensuring that those who protect and live with nature benefit equitably from its services.

**Key Finding**: Economic exploitation and ecological exploitation are inseparable—addressing one requires addressing both. Ecological justice demands economic democracy.

## 1. Economic Inequality and Poverty (2025)

### 1.1 The Numbers Tell the Story

**Income Inequality**:
- **GINI Index**: .545 (Puerto Rico) vs .482 (U.S. average) - highest of any U.S. jurisdiction
- **Only 1.4% of households** earn over $200,000/year
- **25% of households** survive on $10,000/year or less (vs. 6% U.S. average)
- **Median wages**: Approximately half of U.S. mainland levels
- **Middle class**: Shrinking as professionals migrate to mainland

**Poverty Crisis**:
- **43% poverty rate** (1.4 million people living on <$22,000/year)
- **56%+ of children** live in poverty
- **Persistent poverty**: Multi-generational for many families
- **Rural poverty**: Often exceeding 50% in some municipalities
- **Elderly poverty**: Fixed incomes inadequate for rising costs

**Wealth Concentration**:
- **Top 1%**: Luxuriate in gated enclaves, benefiting from Act 60 tax breaks
- **Bottom 43%**: Scraping by with minimal resources
- **Wealth gap**: Extreme and growing
- **Asset inequality**: Land, housing, investments concentrated at top
- **Intergenerational wealth**: Historical patterns of exclusion persist

### 1.2 Colonial Economic Structure

**Historical Extraction**:
- **1898-present**: Colonial economy extracting value to U.S.
- **Export-oriented**: Economy structured to serve external markets
- **Import-dependent**: Food, energy, goods from mainland (enriching external corporations)
- **Capital flight**: Profits leave island rather than circulating locally
- **Debt burden**: $70+ billion limiting local investment

**Fiscal Control and Austerity**:
- **PROMESA (2016)**: Federally-imposed fiscal control board
- **Unelected oversight**: No democratic accountability
- **Austerity measures**: Cuts to education, health, environmental protection
- **Debt prioritized**: Over people and planet
- **Public assets**: Sold or privatized (LUMA Energy, water system proposals)

**Limited Economic Sovereignty**:
- **Jones Act (1920)**: Requiring expensive U.S.-flagged shipping
- **Federal minimum wage**: Not adjusted for higher costs of living on island
- **No control over currency**: Cannot devalue or adjust monetary policy
- **Trade restrictions**: Cannot negotiate independent trade agreements
- **Investment barriers**: Federal policies limiting economic development options

## 2. Who Benefits from Nature? Resource Extraction and Tourism

### 2.1 Tourism: Boom for Whom?

**Tourism Revenue (2025)**:
- **Record growth**: Year-over-year increases for past 4 years
- **2024**: Record tourism year
- **2025 projections**: Expected to surpass 2024
- **Coral reefs alone**: Generate nearly $2 billion annually in reef-related tourism
- **Total tourism**: Multiple billions in economic activity

**Who Captures the Value?**:
- **International hotel chains**: Profits leave Puerto Rico
- **Cruise lines**: Foreign-flagged ships, minimal local spending
- **Airbnb investors**: Often mainland or foreign owners
- **Tour operators**: Many externally-owned
- **Local workers**: Low wages, seasonal employment, limited benefits
- **Communities**: Bear environmental costs, receive minimal benefit

**Tourism-Driven Displacement**:
- **Airbnb boom**: Driving up rents, displacing long-term residents
- **Gentrification**: Wealthy tourists and new residents pricing out locals
- **Coastal privatization**: Beaches and coastline increasingly exclusive
- **Cultural commodification**: Puerto Rican culture as product for tourists
- **Loss of community character**: Traditional neighborhoods transformed

### 2.2 Ecosystem Services: Who Pays, Who Receives?

**Coral Reefs**:
- **Annual value**: $2B+ tourism, $184M storm protection
- **Who maintains**: No one - degrading from lack of investment
- **Who benefits**: Tourism industry, coastal property owners (often wealthy)
- **Who pays for restoration**: Public funds ($40M NOAA 2025) benefiting private tourism
- **Fishers**: Depleted reefs reducing traditional livelihoods

**Mangroves**:
- **Storm protection**: Saving millions in property damage annually
- **Who benefits**: Coastal property owners (disproportionately wealthy)
- **Who maintains**: Degraded until recent restoration efforts
- **Restoration funding**: Public ($146 acres, NFWF grant)
- **Fishing communities**: Benefit from nursery habitat, but overfishing depleting stocks

**Forests (El Yunque)**:
- **Water supply**: ~20% of island population
- **Water as commodity**: Privatization threats (everyone needs water, profit from essential service)
- **Recreation**: Free access currently, but commercialization pressures
- **Carbon storage**: Global benefit, local costs of maintenance
- **Research value**: Scientific insights, but locals often excluded from participation

**Agricultural Land**:
- **600,000 acres**: Designated but largely unused
- **Ownership**: Concentrated in few hands (absentee landlords, corporations)
- **Use**: Speculation, not production
- **Potential**: Could feed 2/3 of population but controlled by those not farming
- **Farmers**: Lack access to land they could productively use

### 2.3 Fishing Rights and Marine Resources

**Traditional Fishing Communities**:
- **Generations of knowledge**: Traditional fishing grounds and practices
- **Loss of access**: Privatization, exclusion, overfishing by industrial operators
- **Resource depletion**: Stocks declining from overexploitation
- **Economic decline**: Fishing communities impoverished
- **Cultural loss**: Fishing traditions disappearing

**Commercial vs. Artisanal**:
- **Industrial operations**: Taking largest share of catch
- **Artisanal fishers**: Squeezed by regulations designed for industrial scale
- **Market power**: Large buyers dictating prices to small fishers
- **Value-added**: Processing and retail profits captured by intermediaries
- **Sustainability**: Small-scale fishing more sustainable but economically marginalized

**Marine Reserves**:
- **Conservation benefit**: Protecting biodiversity
- **Access restrictions**: Limiting traditional fishing grounds
- **No compensation**: Fishers bearing cost of conservation for public benefit
- **Ecotourism**: New actors capturing value from reserves
- **Just transition needed**: Supporting fishers in transition to sustainable practices

## 3. Land Ownership, Gentrification, and Displacement

### 3.1 Act 60 and the Wealthy Migration

**Act 60 (formerly Act 20/22) Tax Incentives**:
- **Capital gains**: 0% tax on capital gains for new residents
- **Corporate tax**: 4% for certain businesses (vs. 37% mainland)
- **Attracts wealthy**: Cryptocurrency investors, hedge fund managers, entrepreneurs
- **Recent reform (June 2025)**: 4% tax imposed on new individual beneficiaries

**Impacts on Housing and Displacement**:
- **Real estate prices**: Skyrocketing in desirable areas
- **Rents**: Unaffordable for locals earning Puerto Rican wages
- **Displacement**: Long-term residents forced out of ancestral neighborhoods
- **Gentrification**: Particularly severe in San Juan, Rincón, Vieques, Culebra
- **Cultural erasure**: Communities broken apart, traditions disrupted

**Environmental Gentrification**:
- **Green infrastructure**: Increasing property values, pricing out residents
- **Beach access**: Wealthy enclaves restricting access to coastline
- **"Eco-tourism" development**: Benefiting investors, displacing communities
- **Conservation lands**: Sometimes used to justify displacement

### 3.2 Land Tenure Insecurity

**Unclear Title**:
- **Rural areas**: Many lack formal property titles
- **Inheritance patterns**: Informal division of family land
- **Vulnerability**: Cannot access credit, government assistance, legal protections
- **Land grabbing**: Easier to take land from those without formal title
- **Post-disaster**: Difficulty accessing recovery aid without proof of ownership

**Corporate Land Ownership**:
- **Agricultural land**: Concentrated ownership
- **Development land**: Speculation driving prices up
- **Extractive uses**: Land for profit, not community benefit
- **Absentee ownership**: Owners not invested in community wellbeing

### 3.3 Housing Crisis and Ecological Impact

**Substandard Housing**:
- **Quality**: Many low-income homes lack basic amenities, hurricane resilience
- **Environmental hazards**: Built in flood zones, contaminated areas
- **Energy inefficiency**: High cooling costs for poorly-insulated homes
- **Health impacts**: Mold, lead, inadequate ventilation

**Development Pressures on Ecosystems**:
- **Coastal construction**: Destroying mangroves, coral reefs, nesting beaches
- **Urban sprawl**: Consuming agricultural land and forests
- **Infrastructure**: Roads, utilities fragmenting habitats
- **Luxury development**: Golf courses, resorts using scarce water, degrading ecosystems

## 4. Conservation: Who Pays, Who Decides?

### 4.1 Conservation Funding Inequity

**Who Funds Conservation**:
- **Taxpayers**: General public paying for conservation through taxes
- **Federal grants**: U.S. taxpayers (PR contributes less per capita due to poverty)
- **Philanthropy**: Wealthy individuals and foundations deciding priorities
- **International NGOs**: External organizations setting agendas
- **Local communities**: Contributing labor and land, often uncompensated

**Who Controls Conservation Decisions**:
- **Government agencies**: Often federal, not local control
- **NGOs**: Board members typically wealthy, not representative of affected communities
- **Scientists**: Important expertise but not always incorporating local knowledge
- **Private landowners**: Conservation on private land controlled by owners
- **Communities**: Often excluded from decision-making affecting their lives

**Benefit Distribution**:
- **Biodiversity**: Global benefit (humanity)
- **Carbon storage**: Global climate benefit
- **Tourism**: Industry profits
- **Local communities**: Bear restrictions and costs, receive minimal compensation
- **Wages**: Conservation jobs often low-paid, temporary

### 4.2 Conservation-Induced Displacement

**"Fortress Conservation" Model**:
- **Exclusion**: Communities removed or restricted from protected areas
- **Loss of livelihoods**: Fishing, farming, gathering restricted
- **No compensation**: People bearing costs of conservation for global benefit
- **Cultural impacts**: Sacred sites, traditional use areas made inaccessible
- **Resistance**: Communities opposing conservation that doesn't benefit them

**Alternative Models Needed**:
- **Community-based conservation**: Local control and benefit
- **Co-management**: Sharing decision-making power
- **Benefit-sharing**: Communities receiving economic benefits from conservation
- **Traditional use rights**: Recognizing and protecting indigenous and traditional practices
- **Payment for ecosystem services**: Compensating communities for conservation

### 4.3 Green Economy: Jobs or Greenwashing?

**Potential of Green Economy**:
- **Renewable energy**: Installation, maintenance jobs
- **Restoration**: Mangrove planting, reforestation, reef restoration
- **Ecotourism**: Guides, education, sustainable operations
- **Sustainable agriculture**: Agroecology providing livelihoods
- **Research**: Scientific and technical positions

**Current Reality**:
- **Low wages**: Many green jobs pay poorly
- **Temporary**: Project-based employment, not career paths
- **Outside contractors**: Jobs often going to mainland workers
- **Limited training**: Locals lacking access to skills development
- **Corporate capture**: Green economy benefiting corporations, not communities

**Just Transition Principles**:
- **Living wages**: Green jobs must pay enough to live on
- **Union rights**: Organize for better conditions
- **Local hiring**: Prioritize affected communities
- **Training**: Accessible skills development
- **Worker cooperatives**: Community ownership of green businesses

## 5. Debt, Austerity, and Environmental Degradation

### 5.1 The $70+ Billion Debt Crisis

**Origins of Debt**:
- **Colonial structure**: Economic inequality generating need for social spending
- **Bond market**: Wall Street profiting from PR bonds
- **Constitutional debt**: Cannot declare bankruptcy like states
- **Compound interest**: Debt growing faster than ability to repay
- **Corruption and mismanagement**: Some funds misused (but debt persists)

**PROMESA and Austerity**:
- **Fiscal Control Board**: Unelected, prioritizing debt over democracy
- **Budget cuts**: Education, health, environmental protection slashed
- **Public sector**: Massive job losses, reduced services
- **Privatization**: Assets sold to pay debt (enriching private corporations)
- **Austerity's impact**: Weakening capacity for environmental protection

### 5.2 Environmental Consequences of Austerity

**Department of Natural Resources (DRNA)**:
- **Budget cuts**: Less funding for conservation, enforcement, monitoring
- **Staff reductions**: Fewer personnel for protection of natural resources
- **Deferred maintenance**: Parks, forests, reserves deteriorating
- **Monitoring**: Less capacity to track environmental changes
- **Enforcement**: Violations going unaddressed due to limited resources

**Infrastructure Decay**:
- **Water systems**: Aging pipes, treatment plants failing
- **Waste management**: Inadequate facilities, illegal dumping increasing
- **Stormwater**: Systems overwhelmed, flooding worsening
- **Climate adaptation**: Deferred investments increasing vulnerability

**Research and Education**:
- **University cuts**: Reduced environmental research and education
- **School programs**: Environmental education eliminated
- **Public outreach**: Limited capacity for conservation education
- **Data collection**: Long-term monitoring programs discontinued

### 5.3 Debt-for-Nature Swaps: Opportunity or Neocolonialism?

**Concept**:
- **Debt reduction**: In exchange for conservation commitments
- **Precedents**: Used in other countries
- **Potential**: Could free funds for both debt relief and conservation

**Concerns**:
- **Sovereignty**: External actors dictating environmental policy
- **Priorities**: Conservation goals set by creditors, not communities
- **Community exclusion**: Deals made without local participation
- **Unequal power**: Creditors with leverage, PR without negotiating power
- **Neocolonialism**: Continuing extractive relationships under green guise

**Principles for Just Debt Relief**:
- **Community voice**: PR communities deciding conservation priorities
- **Debt cancellation**: Not just swaps, but actual debt relief
- **Reparations**: Recognizing debt as result of colonial extraction
- **Benefit-sharing**: Conservation generating local benefits, not just external
- **Self-determination**: PR controlling own environmental and economic policies

## 6. Alternative Economic Models: Toward Ecological Economics

### 6.1 Solidarity Economy and Cooperatives

**Cooperative Models**:
- **Worker cooperatives**: Democratic ownership of businesses
- **Agricultural cooperatives**: Farmers pooling resources and marketing
- **Energy cooperatives**: Community-owned renewable energy
- **Housing cooperatives**: Affordable, community-controlled housing
- **Consumer cooperatives**: Bulk buying, local food systems

**Examples in Puerto Rico**:
- **Cooperative movement**: History of agricultural cooperatives (though weakened)
- **New initiatives**: Renewable energy, food, housing cooperatives emerging
- **Solidarity economy networks**: Alternative to extractive capitalism
- **Benefit**: Profits circulate locally, democratic governance, community resilience

### 6.2 Commons and Community Ownership

**Land Trusts**:
- **Community land trusts**: Permanent affordable housing
- **Agricultural commons**: Shared access to farmland (Las Perdices Farm, Ciales)
- **Forest commons**: Community management of forests
- **Coastal commons**: Collective stewardship of beaches and waters

**Benefits**:
- **Affordability**: Removing land from speculative market
- **Stewardship**: Long-term care rather than short-term profit
- **Community control**: Democratic decision-making
- **Cultural preservation**: Protecting places of community significance

### 6.3 Regenerative Economy

**Principles**:
- **Circular economy**: Waste = food, closed loop systems
- **Biomimicry**: Learning from nature's efficiency
- **Restoration**: Economy that heals rather than harms
- **Well-being**: Human and ecological health as measures of success, not just GDP
- **Local first**: Prioritizing local production and consumption

**Applications**:
- **Agroecology**: Regenerating soils while feeding people
- **Ecosystem restoration**: Creating jobs while healing nature
- **Renewable energy**: Distributed generation, community-owned
- **Circular materials**: Recycling, upcycling, composting
- **Traditional knowledge**: Reclaiming sustainable practices

### 6.4 Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES) - Done Right

**Concept**:
- **Recognize value**: Ecosystems provide services (clean water, carbon storage, etc.)
- **Compensate stewards**: Pay those protecting and maintaining ecosystems
- **Internalize externalities**: Beneficiaries pay for what they receive

**Current Problems**:
- **Commodification**: Nature reduced to dollars
- **Elite capture**: Payments going to large landowners, not communities
- **Greenwashing**: Companies "offsetting" while continuing pollution
- **Colonialism**: Global North paying Global South to not develop

**Justice Principles**:
- **Community control**: Local people deciding and managing
- **Fair compensation**: Adequate payment for stewardship
- **Rights-based**: Recognizing inherent value of nature, not just instrumental
- **Additionality**: Payments for conservation beyond legal requirements
- **Transparency**: Clear accounting of who pays and receives

## 7. Tourism Justice: Sustainable, Equitable, Community-Controlled

### 7.1 Problems with Current Tourism Model

**Extractive Tourism**:
- **Profits leave**: International chains, cruise lines, external investors
- **Environmental degradation**: Overdevelopment, pollution, resource depletion
- **Cultural commodification**: Authenticity manufactured for tourists
- **Labor exploitation**: Low wages, poor conditions for service workers
- **Displacement**: Residents priced out of tourist areas

**Cruise Ship Impact**:
- **Limited local benefit**: Passengers don't spend much on-island
- **Environmental harm**: Air and water pollution from ships
- **Congestion**: Overwhelming small towns (Old San Juan)
- **Foreign-flagged**: Avoiding U.S. labor and environmental laws

### 7.2 Community-Based Ecotourism

**Principles**:
- **Community ownership**: Locals controlling and benefiting from tourism
- **Small-scale**: Appropriate to local capacity and environment
- **Educational**: Visitors learning, not just consuming
- **Conservation-linked**: Tourism revenue supporting protection
- **Cultural respect**: Authentic exchange, not performance

**Examples**:
- **Community-led tours**: Local guides sharing knowledge and culture
- **Homestays**: Visitors staying with families, direct economic benefit
- **Cooperative lodging**: Community-owned accommodations
- **Agritourism**: Farm visits supporting agroecology
- **Conservation tourism**: Visitors participating in restoration work

### 7.3 Revenue Redistribution

**Tourism Tax**:
- **Progressive taxation**: Luxury tourism taxed higher
- **Revenue allocation**: Funds for local infrastructure, conservation, community development
- **Community control**: Locals deciding how tourism revenue spent
- **Benefit-sharing**: All residents benefiting from tourism, not just industry

**Local Procurement**:
- **Buy local**: Hotels, restaurants purchasing from local farmers, artisans
- **Hire local**: Employment prioritized for residents
- **Services**: Local businesses providing tours, transport, activities
- **Multiplier effect**: Tourism dollars circulating in local economy

## 8. Intellectual Property, Biopiracy, and Traditional Knowledge

### 8.1 Genetic Resources and Pharmaceuticals

**Biodiversity as Resource**:
- **Unique species**: Endemic plants, animals with unique compounds
- **Pharmaceutical potential**: Traditional medicinal plants studied for drug development
- **Who owns**: Genetic resources, traditional knowledge

**Biopiracy Concerns**:
- **Bioprospecting**: Corporations collecting genetic resources
- **Patents**: Companies patenting compounds based on traditional knowledge
- **Profit**: Huge revenues from drugs, communities receive nothing
- **Access**: Expensive drugs unaffordable for communities who provided knowledge

### 8.2 Traditional Knowledge Protection

**Taíno and Local Knowledge**:
- **Medicinal plants**: Generations of knowledge about healing properties
- **Agricultural practices**: Sustainable farming wisdom
- **Ecological knowledge**: Understanding ecosystems and species
- **Spiritual connections**: Sacred relationships with nature

**Threats**:
- **Knowledge loss**: Elders passing without transmitting knowledge
- **Appropriation**: External actors using knowledge without attribution or compensation
- **Intellectual property**: Patent system not recognizing collective, traditional knowledge
- **Documentation**: Risks making knowledge accessible for exploitation

**Protection Mechanisms**:
- **Community protocols**: FPIC (Free, Prior, Informed Consent) requirements
- **Benefit-sharing agreements**: Communities receiving fair compensation
- **Traditional knowledge databases**: Defensive protection
- **Cultural heritage laws**: Legal recognition and protection
- **Community governance**: Local control over access and use

## 9. Climate Finance and Green Colonialism

### 9.1 Climate Adaptation Funding Inequity

**Need vs. Allocation**:
- **PR vulnerability**: High climate exposure, limited resources
- **Federal funding**: Less per capita than states
- **Private investment**: Flowing to profitable "green" projects, not community needs
- **International climate finance**: PR not eligible (not independent nation)

**Who Controls Climate Investments**:
- **Fiscal control board**: Prioritizing debt over adaptation
- **Federal agencies**: Decisions made in Washington, not San Juan
- **Private investors**: Seeking returns, not serving community needs
- **Communities**: Excluded from decisions about their own future

### 9.2 Green Grabbing

**Land for Conservation/Renewables**:
- **Large-scale solar**: Corporate projects displacing agriculture or communities
- **Wind farms**: Taking land, minimal community benefit
- **Forest offsets**: Restricting community use for carbon credits sold abroad
- **Coastal rezoning**: "Climate adaptation" justifying displacement

**Preventing Green Grabbing**:
- **Community land rights**: Secure tenure preventing displacement
- **Participatory planning**: Communities deciding on green infrastructure location
- **Benefit-sharing**: Local ownership or revenue from renewable energy
- **Just transition**: Support for affected communities

## 10. Policy Recommendations for Economic Justice

### 10.1 Structural Changes

**Debt and Sovereignty**:
1. **Debt audit and cancellation**: Illegitimate debt relief
2. **End PROMESA**: Democratic control over budget
3. **Political status resolution**: Statehood or independence
4. **Repeal Jones Act**: Reduce shipping costs
5. **Economic self-determination**: Control over economic policy

**Land Reform**:
1. **Redistribute idle land**: Access for farmers
2. **Community land trusts**: Permanent affordability
3. **Protect traditional tenure**: Recognize informal land rights
4. **Prevent speculation**: Tax vacant land, limit foreign ownership
5. **Act 60 reform**: Progressive taxation of wealthy newcomers (expanded beyond 2025 reform)

### 10.2 Economic Democracy

**Cooperative Development**:
1. **Financing**: Public support for cooperative businesses
2. **Technical assistance**: Training in cooperative governance
3. **Preferential procurement**: Government buying from cooperatives
4. **Legal framework**: Laws supporting cooperative formation
5. **Federation**: Network of cooperatives for mutual support

**Community Ownership**:
1. **Energy cooperatives**: Community-owned renewables
2. **Food hubs**: Cooperative distribution infrastructure
3. **Housing cooperatives**: Affordable, community-controlled
4. **Worker ownership**: Support conversion to worker cooperatives

### 10.3 Fair Benefit-Sharing

**Ecosystem Services**:
1. **Payment to communities**: Compensate conservation stewards
2. **Tourism revenue**: Progressive tax funding community development
3. **Fishing quotas**: Prioritize artisanal fishers
4. **Forest products**: Community rights to sustainable harvest
5. **Genetic resources**: Benefit-sharing agreements for bioprospecting

**Conservation Jobs**:
1. **Living wages**: Green jobs must support families
2. **Career paths**: Training and advancement opportunities
3. **Local hiring**: Priority for affected communities
4. **Union rights**: Organize for better conditions
5. **Community enterprises**: Local ownership of conservation businesses

### 10.4 Progressive Taxation

**Tax Justice**:
1. **Wealth tax**: Tax extreme wealth concentration
2. **Progressive income tax**: Higher earners pay fair share
3. **Corporate tax**: Close loopholes, ensure payment
4. **Luxury tax**: Tax high-end tourism, goods, services
5. **Land value tax**: Discourage speculation
6. **Carbon tax**: Polluters pay (with protections for low-income)

- **Cooperative economy**: Democratic ownership of businesses

## Conclusion

Economic justice and ecological justice are inseparable. Puerto Rico's extraordinary natural wealth—coral reefs generating $2B+ annually, mangroves protecting coasts, forests supplying water, biodiversity supporting tourism—exists within a profoundly unjust economic system. Wealth concentrates at the top (GINI .545, 1.4% earning >$200K), while 43% live in poverty. Nature's benefits flow to corporations and the wealthy, while communities bearing environmental costs receive minimal compensation.

True conservation requires transforming this economic injustice. It means:
- **Community ownership** of natural resources and green economy
- **Fair benefit-sharing** from ecosystem services
- **Living wages** for conservation and green jobs
- **Land reform** for farmers and communities
- **Debt relief** freeing resources for people and planet
- **Cooperative economy** circulating wealth locally
- **Economic self-determination** controlling destiny

**The vision is clear: An economy that regenerates rather than extracts, that distributes wealth fairly, that recognizes nature's value, and that ensures everyone—not just the elite—benefits from Borikén's extraordinary natural heritage. Economic justice is ecological justice. We cannot have one without the other.**
