# Invasive Species in Borikén (Puerto Rico) - 2025 Status and Management

## Overview

Invasive species represent one of the most severe and persistent threats to Puerto Rico's native biodiversity. As an isolated island ecosystem, Borikén's native species evolved without exposure to many continental predators, competitors, and diseases, leaving them particularly vulnerable to introduced organisms. As of 2025, invasive species are contributing significantly to the decline of endemic species and the degradation of ecosystems across the island.

## Major Invasive Species by Category

### Invasive Mammals

#### Small Indian Mongoose (*Urva auropunctata*)
**Introduction**: 1877, deliberately introduced for sugarcane rat control
**Status**: Established throughout island, one of world's 100 worst invasive species

**Impacts**:
- **Native reptiles**: Severe predation on ground-dwelling lizards, snakes (including Puerto Rican Boa)
- **Endemic birds**: Nest predation, especially ground-nesting and low-nesting species
- **Native amphibians**: Predation on coquí frogs and other amphibians
- **Ecological cascade**: Reduction of native predators allowing invasive species to thrive
- **Disease vector**: Rabies carrier (though rabies-free status maintained through vaccination programs)

**Why it persists**:
- Highly adaptable, reproduces rapidly
- Diurnal activity means it doesn't overlap temporally with target rats (which are nocturnal)
- Effective predator with few natural enemies in Puerto Rico
- Difficult and expensive to control at landscape scale

**Current management**:
- Trapping programs in critical endangered species habitats
- Predator-proof fencing around Puerto Rican Parrot release sites
- Research on contraceptive methods
- Public education about not releasing or relocating mongooses

#### Feral Cats (*Felis catus*)
**Status**: Widespread across urban, suburban, and natural areas

**Impacts**:
- **Bird predation**: Estimates suggest cats kill millions of birds annually in Puerto Rico
- **Endemic species**: Particular threat to endemic birds, reptiles, and small mammals
- **Disease transmission**: Toxoplasmosis affecting wildlife and humans
- **Ecosystem disruption**: Suppressing native prey populations

**Management challenges**:
- High public attachment to cats
- Colony feeding increasing populations
- Reproduction rate exceeds control efforts
- Conflict between animal welfare and conservation perspectives

**Current approaches**:
- Trap-Neuter-Return (TNR) programs in urban areas (controversial for conservation)
- Education about keeping cats indoors
- Removal programs in critical habitats (nature reserves)
- Vaccination programs for disease control

#### Rats (Multiple species)
**Species**:
- Black rat (*Rattus rattus*)
- Norway rat (*Rattus norvegicus*)

**Impacts**:
- **Seed predation**: Consuming native tree seeds, preventing forest regeneration
- **Nest predation**: Eating eggs and chicks of endemic birds
- **Crop damage**: Agricultural pest
- **Native species competition**: Outcompeting native small mammals
- **Disease**: Leptospirosis and other zoonotic diseases

**Management**:
- Rodenticides in strategic locations (with precautions for non-target species)
- Trapping programs
- Predator-proof nest boxes for endangered birds
- Forest regeneration programs accounting for rat predation

#### Rhesus Macaque Monkeys (*Macaca mulatta*)
**Status**: Small populations on Cayo Santiago (research colony) and occasionally escaping

**Concerns**:
- Potential crop raiders if population expands
- Disease transmission risk (including to humans)
- Competition with native wildlife
- Currently contained but requires vigilance

### Invasive Reptiles

#### Green Iguana (*Iguana iguana*)
**Introduction**: Pet trade releases and escapes
**Status**: Rapidly expanding populations throughout Puerto Rico

**Impacts**:
- **Native plant consumption**: Heavy browsing on native vegetation
- **Competition**: Outcompeting native herbivores for food and space
- **Tree damage**: Heavy animals breaking branches
- **Hybridization risk**: Potential crossbreeding with any native iguanas (though none currently present)
- **Infrastructure**: Burrowing under roads and structures
- **Ecosystem alteration**: Changing plant community composition through selective feeding

**Current status (2025)**:
Populations have expanded dramatically in recent years. Major concern for native biodiversity and ecosystem functioning.

**Management efforts**:
- Hunting programs (including culinary promotion)
- Trapping initiatives
- Public education about not releasing pets
- Research on population control methods
- Some communities organizing iguana removal efforts

#### Other Non-Native Reptiles
- Various escaped pet reptiles establishing small populations
- Constant monitoring needed to prevent establishment

### Invasive Amphibians

#### Coquí Eleutherodactylus coqui in Hawaii
Note: While Puerto Rico's coquí is native and beloved, it has become invasive in Hawaii, demonstrating how context determines invasiveness. This serves as a reminder that species can be native treasures in one location and problematic invaders in another.

#### Marine Toad/Cane Toad (*Rhinella marina*)
**Status**: Present but not yet widespread
**Concerns**:
- Toxic to predators
- Potential impact on native amphibians through competition
- Large, voracious appetite

### Invasive Fish

#### Tilapia (Multiple species)
**Introduction**: Aquaculture escapes
**Status**: Established in freshwater systems

**Impacts**:
- Competition with native freshwater fish
- Ecosystem disruption through bottom-feeding
- Potential disease transmission

### Invasive Marine Species

#### Indo-Pacific Lionfish (*Pterois volitans/miles*)
**Introduction**: Aquarium trade releases in Florida; natural dispersal to Caribbean
**Status**: Well-established throughout Puerto Rico's coastal waters by 2025

**Impacts**:
- **Voracious predator**: Consuming native reef fish, including juveniles of commercial species
- **No natural predators**: Caribbean predators haven't evolved to recognize and eat lionfish
- **Rapid reproduction**: Females can produce ~2 million eggs per year
- **Ecosystem disruption**: Altering reef fish communities and food webs
- **Competition**: Outcompeting native predators for prey
- **Venomous**: Painful stings to humans, though not usually fatal

**Current management (2025)**:
- **Organized lionfish derbies**: Community events to catch and remove lionfish
- **Culinary promotion**: "Eat the invader" campaigns encouraging consumption
  - Lionfish are excellent eating (white, flaky meat)
  - Safe when properly filleted (venom in spines, not flesh)
  - Restaurants serving lionfish to create demand
- **Trained divers**: Spearfishing programs removing lionfish from reefs
- **Education**: Teaching divers to recognize and safely handle lionfish
- **Research**: Studying lionfish ecology and developing control methods
- **Reality**: Complete eradication unlikely; ongoing management required

**Success and challenges**:
- Local reductions achieved through intensive efforts
- Requires sustained, costly control to maintain benefits
- Deep reef populations harder to control
- Lionfish continue to reproduce and disperse

### Invasive Plants

Puerto Rico hosts hundreds of invasive plant species. Key examples include:

#### African Tulip Tree (*Spathodea campanulata*)
**Status**: Widespread, particularly in disturbed forests

**Impacts**:
- Outcompeting native trees
- Altering forest composition
- Reducing native plant diversity
- Potentially toxic to native pollinators

#### Albizia / Tall Albizia (*Falcataria moluccana*)
**Status**: Very fast-growing, spreading rapidly

**Impacts**:
- Rapid colonization of disturbed areas
- Outcompeting native successional species
- Weak wood makes it vulnerable to storm damage
- Nitrogen-fixing alters soil chemistry

#### Bamboo Species
**Status**: Multiple species established, spreading

**Impacts**:
- Forms dense monocultures excluding native plants
- Difficult to remove once established
- Alters soil and water dynamics

#### Australian Pine (*Casuarina equisetifolia*)
**Status**: Common in coastal areas

**Impacts**:
- Displacing native beach vegetation
- Altering dune structure
- Leaf litter inhibits native plant germination
- Provides poor wildlife habitat compared to natives

#### Melaleuca (*Melaleuca quinquenervia*)
**Status**: Localized but problematic where present

**Impacts**:
- Forms monocultures in wetlands
- Alters hydrology
- Fire-adapted, increasing fire risk in tropical systems

#### Guinea Grass (*Megathyrsus maximus*)
**Status**: Widespread in open areas

**Impacts**:
- Fuel for fires (unusual in tropical wet forests)
- Preventing native forest regeneration
- Converting forest to grassland
- Reducing native plant diversity

#### Ivy Gourd (*Coccinia grandis*)
**Status**: Rapidly spreading vine

**Impacts**:
- Smothering native vegetation
- Particularly problematic in forest edges and disturbed areas
- Difficult to control

### Invasive Invertebrates

#### Giant African Snail (*Lissachatina fulica*)
**Status**: Periodically detected, eradication efforts deployed

**Concerns**:
- Agricultural pest (consumes crops)
- Disease vector (rat lungworm)
- Environmental impacts on native snails and plants
- Rapid reproduction if not controlled

**Management**:
- Early detection and rapid response critical
- Public reporting programs
- Hand collection and destruction
- Quarantine measures

#### Fire Ants (Multiple species)
**Status**: Several species established

**Impacts**:
- Painful stings to humans and wildlife
- Predation on native invertebrates
- Disrupting native ant communities and ecosystem functions
- Affecting ground-nesting birds

#### Asian Citrus Psyllid and Other Agricultural Pests
**Status**: Various invasive agricultural pests established

**Impacts**:
- Crop damage
- Disease vectors (e.g., citrus greening disease)
- Economic losses
- Pesticide use impacting non-target species

### Invasive Diseases and Pathogens

#### Chytrid Fungus (*Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*)
**Status**: Present in Puerto Rico, affecting amphibian populations

**Impacts on coquí frogs (2025)**:
- **Increased prevalence**: Rising temperatures have increased infection rates
- **Reduced reproduction**: Infected frogs have lower reproductive success
- **Increased mortality**: Particularly in high-elevation species
- **Population declines**: Mountain Coquí (*Eleutherodactylus portoricensis*) experiencing declines partially attributed to chytrid
- **Range restrictions**: May limit where species can survive
- **Interaction with climate change**: Warming creating more favorable conditions for fungus

**Management challenges**:
- Difficult to control in wild populations
- No effective treatment at landscape scale
- Climate change exacerbating problem
- Focus on maintaining disease-free populations and habitat protection

#### Avian Diseases
- Various diseases affecting endemic birds
- Increased transmission with climate change and stress

#### Coral Diseases
- **Stony Coral Tissue Loss Disease (SCTLD)**: Devastating corals (see coastal/marine ecosystems document)
- Multiple other coral diseases affecting reef health

## Pathways of Introduction

Understanding how invasive species arrive helps prevent future invasions:

### Intentional Introductions (Historical)
- **Mongoose**: Deliberate for supposed pest control (failed, became pest itself)
- **Agricultural animals**: Escaped livestock and aquaculture
- **Ornamental plants**: Intentionally planted, then escaped cultivation
- **Biological control**: Organisms released to control other pests (often becoming problems themselves)

### Unintentional Introductions
- **Cargo ships**: Ballast water, cargo hitchhikers, hull fouling
- **Air travel**: Insects in cargo, passengers' luggage
- **Nursery trade**: Invasive plants and associated insects
- **Agricultural imports**: Pests arriving with produce
- **Natural dispersal**: From nearby invaded areas (e.g., lionfish from Florida)

### Ongoing Pathways (2025)
- **Pet trade**: Releases and escapes (reptiles, fish, invertebrates)
- **Aquarium releases**: Marine and freshwater species
- **Aquaculture**: Escapes from fish farms
- **Ornamental horticulture**: Garden escapes
- **Internet commerce**: Online plant and animal sales
- **Climate change**: Enabling survival of previously non-viable species

## Ecosystem-Level Impacts

### Forest Ecosystems
- **Altered composition**: Invasive trees and vines changing species mix
- **Reduced regeneration**: Seed predation and competition preventing native plant recruitment
- **Fire regime changes**: Invasive grasses increasing fire frequency and intensity
- **Predation on fauna**: Mongoose, cats, and rats reducing native wildlife
- **Disease**: Pathogens affecting plants and animals

### Marine Ecosystems
- **Reef degradation**: Lionfish reducing native fish populations
- **Food web disruption**: Invasive predators and competitors altering ecosystem structure
- **Coral disease**: Pathogens contributing to coral decline
- **Ballast water introductions**: Risk of future invasions

### Freshwater Ecosystems
- **Fish community changes**: Invasive fish outcompeting natives
- **Habitat modification**: Some invasive plants and animals altering physical environment
- **Disease introduction**: Pathogens affecting native amphibians and fish

### Coastal Ecosystems
- **Beach vegetation changes**: Invasive plants replacing native dune species
- **Predation on sea turtles**: Mongooses and dogs taking turtle eggs
- **Habitat degradation**: Invasive plants providing poor wildlife habitat

## Management Strategies and Challenges

### Prevention
**Most cost-effective approach**: Preventing invasions is far cheaper than controlling established populations.

**Current prevention efforts**:
- **Border inspection**: Agricultural and wildlife inspections at ports and airports
- **Regulations**: Laws prohibiting import of high-risk species
- **Public education**: Awareness campaigns about not releasing pets or plants
- **Early detection systems**: Monitoring for new invaders
- **Rapid response**: Quick action when new species detected

**Challenges**:
- Limited enforcement resources
- Difficulty monitoring all entry points
- Legal pet trade creates invasion risk
- Climate change enabling new species to survive

### Early Detection and Rapid Response (EDRR)
**Concept**: Finding and eliminating invasive species when populations are still small and manageable.

**Components**:
- Public reporting systems (e.g., report suspicious species)
- Trained surveyors monitoring high-risk areas
- Rapid identification capabilities
- Quick-response eradication teams
- Sufficient funding for immediate action

**Challenges**:
- Requires sustained funding
- Public participation essential
- Some species hard to detect when rare
- Political will for rapid action sometimes lacking

### Control and Management
For established invasive species, ongoing management is necessary:

#### Mechanical/Physical Control
- **Trapping**: Mongooses, cats, rats
- **Hand removal**: Plants, snails
- **Hunting**: Iguanas, lionfish
- **Pulling/Cutting**: Invasive plants

**Advantages**: No chemicals, can be targeted
**Disadvantages**: Labor-intensive, expensive, requires ongoing effort

#### Chemical Control
- **Rodenticides**: For rats (risks to non-target species)
- **Herbicides**: For invasive plants (environmental concerns)
- **Pesticides**: For invasive insects

**Advantages**: Effective for some species, can treat large areas
**Disadvantages**: Non-target impacts, environmental concerns, public opposition, repeated applications needed

#### Biological Control
- **Concept**: Using natural enemies to control invasive species
- **Examples**: Pathogens, predators, or parasites specific to target species
- **Extreme caution needed**: History of biological control agents becoming invasive themselves
- **Current use in Puerto Rico**: Limited due to risks

#### Integrated Pest Management (IPM)
- **Combining approaches**: Using multiple control methods strategically
- **Adapting to conditions**: Different techniques for different situations
- **Monitoring**: Assessing effectiveness and adjusting strategies
- **Best practice**: Generally most effective approach

### Restoration
After controlling invasive species, active restoration often needed:
- **Native plant reintroduction**: Planting native species to prevent reinvasion
- **Habitat restoration**: Recreating conditions favoring natives
- **Protected areas**: Preventing reintroduction of invasives
- **Long-term monitoring**: Ensuring invasives don't return

### Challenges to Management

#### Resource Limitations
- **Insufficient funding**: Control programs chronically underfunded
- **Limited personnel**: Not enough staff for needed control efforts
- **Competing priorities**: Resources needed for multiple conservation issues

#### Biological Challenges
- **High reproduction rates**: Many invasives reproduce faster than control efforts
- **Multiple introduction sources**: Reintroduction from outside controlled areas
- **Genetic diversity**: Invasive populations adapting to control efforts
- **Habitat preferences**: Some species difficult to access for control

#### Social Challenges
- **Public attachment**: Some people like certain invasive species (e.g., cats, parrots)
- **Lack of awareness**: Many people don't understand invasive species impacts
- **Property rights**: Control efforts complicated on private land
- **Conflicting values**: Animal welfare vs. conservation conflicts

#### Political Challenges
- **Short-term thinking**: Invasive species control requires long-term commitment
- **Lack of political priority**: Other issues may receive more attention
- **Regulatory gaps**: Inadequate laws or enforcement for some invasive species
- **Jurisdictional issues**: Multiple agencies with overlapping authorities

## Economic Costs of Invasive Species

### Direct Costs
- **Agricultural damage**: Crop losses from invasive pests - millions annually
- **Infrastructure damage**: Termites, iguanas - millions in repairs
- **Control programs**: Government spending on management
- **Disease costs**: Healthcare for invasive-vectored diseases

### Indirect Costs
- **Ecosystem service loss**: Reduced water quality, pollination, storm protection
- **Biodiversity loss**: Extinction of native species (irreplaceable)
- **Tourism impacts**: Degraded natural attractions
- **Fisheries decline**: Reduced catches due to invasive species impacts

### Total Economic Impact
Comprehensive economic analysis of invasive species costs in Puerto Rico lacking, but likely hundreds of millions to billions of dollars over past century.

## Success Stories and Hope

Despite challenges, some invasive species management efforts show success:

### Giant African Snail Eradication Efforts
- Rapid response to detections preventing establishment
- Community engagement in reporting
- Demonstrates value of early detection and rapid response

### Lionfish Control in Specific Reefs
- Intensive removal efforts reducing local populations
- Demonstrating that sustained effort can provide benefits
- Creating economic opportunity (lionfish as food)

### Invasive Plant Removal in Reserves
- Native forest recovery following removal of invasive trees
- Demonstrates that restoration is possible with sufficient effort
- Protected areas providing focus for control efforts

### Public Education Advances
- Growing awareness of invasive species issues
- More people reporting suspicious species
- "Don't Let It Loose" campaigns gaining traction
- Increased support for control programs

## Future Outlook and Recommendations

### Emerging Threats
- **Climate change**: Enabling survival of new potential invaders
- **Increased global trade**: More pathways and propagule pressure
- **Novel ecosystems**: Invasive-dominated communities may be permanent
- **Interaction effects**: Multiple invasives and stressors compounding impacts

### Priority Actions for 2025-2030

#### 1. Strengthen Prevention
- Increase border inspection capacity
- Update regulations for emerging threats
- Public education campaigns
- Pathway risk assessments

#### 2. Enhance Early Detection
- Expand monitoring networks
- Improve public reporting systems
- Rapid identification capabilities
- Adequate response funding

#### 3. Strategic Control
- Prioritize efforts by impact and feasibility
- Focus on protecting most valuable habitats
- Control invasives threatening most endangered species
- Invest in research on effective methods

#### 4. Restore Native Ecosystems
- Active restoration after invasive removal
- Native plant propagation and outplanting
- Assisted recovery of native wildlife
- Long-term monitoring and maintenance

#### 5. Build Capacity
- Train more invasive species managers
- Increase funding for control programs
- Develop partnerships with communities
- Integrate invasive species management across agencies

#### 6. Research Priorities
- Ecology of key invasive species
- Effective, low-impact control methods
- Ecosystem restoration techniques
- Economic valuation of impacts and benefits
- Climate change and invasive species interactions

#### 7. Community Engagement
- Education in schools and communities
- Volunteer opportunities in control programs
- Citizen science for early detection
- Cultural connections to native species conservation

### Vision for Success
While complete eradication of most established invasive species is unrealistic, success would include:
- **Prevention**: Greatly reduced rate of new invasions
- **Early detection**: Rapid identification and response to new invaders
- **Strategic control**: Key areas and species protected from worst invasives
- **Native recovery**: Endemic species thriving in protected and restored habitats
- **Public engagement**: Community understanding and support for efforts
- **Adequate resources**: Sufficient funding and personnel for sustained programs

## Conclusion

Invasive species represent one of the greatest threats to Puerto Rico's unique biodiversity and ecosystem functioning. From mongooses devastating endemic reptiles and birds, to invasive plants transforming forests, to lionfish disrupting coral reefs, non-native species are altering Borikén's ecosystems at every level.

However, with adequate resources, strategic prioritization, community engagement, and sustained commitment, the impacts of invasive species can be mitigated. Prevention of new invasions, early detection and rapid response, strategic control of established invaders, and restoration of native ecosystems provide a roadmap for protecting Puerto Rico's natural heritage.

Success requires viewing invasive species management not as a one-time project but as an ongoing commitment—much like maintaining infrastructure or providing education. The alternative—continued biodiversity loss, ecosystem degradation, and economic costs—is unacceptable for a island whose identity is deeply connected to its unique natural environment.
